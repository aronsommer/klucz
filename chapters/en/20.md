# Chapter 20. The Decision

_Saturday, 6 February 2027_

I've been at home for two weeks. I walk on crutches. I mean: I try to walk. Kraków isn't a city for people on crutches. The pavements are crooked, the kerbs are high, the trams have steps, and there's no lift in our tenement. The stairs to the second floor have forty-two steps. I counted. On the first day it took me twenty minutes to get up.

But nobody has ever looked after me so well.

The neighbours drew up a rota among themselves. Nobody asked my opinion. On Mondays and Thursdays Ola brings me lunch: soup, a main course and kompot. On Tuesdays Mr Henryk brings the newspapers and tells me what's in them, although I can read them myself. Mrs Grażyna comes straight after him and tells me what's really in them. On Wednesdays Mrs Danuta comes with two jars of bigos and checks whether I've eaten the previous ones. On Fridays Mr Zbyszek does my shopping and while he's at it repairs something that wasn't broken. And Auntie Halina comes every day, sits in the kitchen and makes sure I do my leg exercises.

Bonifacy has moved in with me for good. He sleeps on my plaster cast. The doctor says it doesn't help. Bonifacy thinks otherwise.

Bonifacy is enormous, rude and sometimes disgusting: last week he was sick on my laptop. But when my leg hurts, he comes, lies down next to me and purrs. Auntie says it's his physiotherapy.

In the evening he flopped onto my bed and slept for ten hours without moving.

I move around the flat on crutches like a pirate on deck. You can hear me coming from far away: tap, tap, tap. I keep away from the stairs. I rarely leave the flat. Usually only once every few days, to the bakery, and that's my expedition of the day.

In the evenings I flick through the TV channels. On one channel they're showing a political debate, on another a quiz show where a player can win a million, on a third a match in which someone has just scored a goal in the last second. Victory! The whole tenement shouts through the walls.

During these weeks I've managed to do things I hadn't done for years. I arranged all of Grandma's books by author. I washed and packed her clothes for people from the parish. I went through all her papers. I cleaned the windows, which nobody had done for a long time. Auntie admired my work and said I was like her dad: when he was ill, he was always tidying up too.

Things I've learnt walking on crutches:

1. It's not worth hurrying.
2. You mustn't take risks on a wet bathroom floor.
3. You can't carry coffee.
4. People are glad to help if you ask them.
5. A cat never helps.

Kasia now comes to my place for the lessons. Last week we studied verbs with prefixes. This, says Kasia, is “Polish magic”: one verb and ten meanings.

— Take “lecieć”, to fly — she said. — Samolot poleciał do Hamburga: the plane flew off to Hamburg. Przeleciał nad Krakowem: it flew over Kraków. Przyleciał na czas: it arrived on time. Odleciał z opóźnieniem: it left late.

— And “jechać”?

— Dojechać do celu: to reach your destination. Przejechać przez most: to cross a bridge. Zjechać ze stoku: to go down a slope, in your case literally. And watch out: “wpakować się” means to get into trouble. You got yourself into trouble on skis.

— And “nieść”, to carry?

— Przynieść, wynieść, wnieść, zanieść, przenieść: bring, carry out, carry in, take somewhere, move. And next to that “nosić” and “przenosić”. Every prefix adds something new. “Prze-” often means through something or to the end: przespać noc, sleep through the night, przejrzeć dokumenty, look through documents, przekroczyć granicę, cross a border, przebić ścianę, break through a wall.

— My brain is about to be destroyed.

— Imagine they're building blocks. Your brain can manage.

Then she pushed back her chair, stood up and made us some tea.

To stop myself going mad, I listen to the radio. Polish radio in the morning is a good lesson: fast, clear and about everything. I wrote down the news from one day:

> “Good morning, it's eight o'clock. Today the president met the prime minister about the budget. The finance minister announced that taxes won't go up, and the opposition claims that's a lie. The elections are in half a year, the campaign is already under way. Around the world: in Brussels a conference on the energy crisis is continuing. Gas and fuel prices are going up again, experts warn of another wave of rises. Economy: production in the car industry has fallen by five per cent. At home: on the A4 motorway near Katowice three cars and a lorry collided. Fortunately nobody was killed, but traffic towards Kraków is blocked. Sport: yesterday in the league Wisła Kraków lost to Legia Warszawa two nil. The Wisła coach said his players played well, but were unlucky. The fans see it differently. Weather: sunny in the south of the country, sleet in the north. Temperatures from minus five to plus three degrees. Drivers, take care: the roads may be slippery.”

Mr Henryk says the news is the only series he has been watching for fifty years. “Always the same script,” he says. “Only the actors change.”

The next day the news was worse: at an airport in one of the European countries the police arrested a terrorist with explosives. There was no explosion, nobody was killed. That country's ambassador gave assurances that his government is cooperating with international organisations. Experts say the threat of attacks in Europe is greater than ever, and every conflict in the world affects our security.

---

The most important thing happened a week ago on Friday, the twenty-ninth of January. The last day on which I could give Michael an answer.

All month I had been calculating. I made a table on the computer, as befits a programmer. On the left: Hamburg. On the right: Kraków.

Hamburg: a higher salary. The post of head of department. A company car. The flat I'm still renting. Friends I've hardly spoken to for three months. A language I know. Rain.

Kraków: lower earnings, if Michael agrees at all to let me keep working remotely. Lower living costs, but not as low as I thought. A coffee in the café downstairs costs almost as much as in Hamburg. Grandma's flat. A tenement that in half a year might belong to Zieliński. An auntie I've known for four months and who is the only family I have in Europe. A cat that isn't mine. A language I'm still learning. And Kasia.

The table didn't solve anything. Tables never solve anything when it comes to life. Grandma would have known that.

On Thursday evening I sat in the kitchen and looked at the note from the dictionary. Grandma's four words. I love. I miss. I'm sorry. I forgive. I thought that for fifty years Grandma lived in Hamburg and missed Kraków. And that I don't want to live in Hamburg for fifty years missing Kraków.

On Friday morning I called Michael.

— Thank you for the offer — I said. In German, but in my head I was thinking in Polish. — I won't accept it. I'm staying in Kraków.

Michael was silent for a long time.

— Are you sure?

— Yes.

— Is it because of a girl?

— Not only. It's because of… — I searched for the word. I couldn't find it in German. — Because of family.

— But you don't have any family there.

— I do. It turned out I do.

— Think about it some more — he added. — I'll call you back tomorrow.

— You don't need to. I've already made the decision.

— People make decisions at night and regret them in the morning.

— I won't regret it in the morning. I can't wait for spring in Kraków.

Michael sighed. Then he said the company doesn't want to lose me and I can go on working remotely, but not as an employee, on a contract with my own company. Less money, more freedom, no company car.

— Deal — I said.

— I knew you'd say that. — I could hear he was smiling. — You know what's strangest? Your German already sounds a bit like Polish.

— Is that good or bad? — I asked. — Good. I'll send you the contract by email. The paperwork will take a week.

---

Setting up a company in Poland is an adventure of its own. Kasia said a sole proprietorship is the simplest form and everything can be done online. Kasia was only half right.

Online I submitted an application to CEIDG, that is the business register. That was easy. Then it turned out I had to choose a form of taxation: the tax scale, flat-rate tax, lump-sum tax. I have to register with ZUS, the Social Insurance Institution, and pay contributions every month: pension, disability, sickness, accident, health. I should have a separate business account at the bank. I have to issue invoices. I have to have an accountant.

Mrs Danuta found the accountant. Of course. It's Mrs Ewa, a friend of hers from school, who has run an accounting office in Podgórze for thirty years. Mrs Ewa came to me with a folder of forms, explained everything to me in an hour and at the end said:

— Don't worry. Polish regulations are difficult, but logical. I mean: they aren't logical. But I know them.

Then Mrs Ewa worked everything out in five minutes: income, costs, tax, contributions, in other words how much is left for me. Less was left than I thought, but more than I feared.

— Enough for bread — she said. — For butter too, if you don't buy the one in gold paper.

Mrs Ewa is the best financial adviser I've ever had. She explained that as an entrepreneur I have to set my own hourly rate and issue an invoice every month. That I can employ someone, but for now there's no need. That a free bank account is never really free, and a credit card is a man's most expensive friend.

— And what will my profit be? — I asked.

— Mr Jonas, in Poland profit is what's left after ZUS. But every grosz counts.

Michael has already offered me an extra project for the spring. I had two options: accept it and work at night, or refuse and sleep. I chose the first option. Of course. I've sublet my flat in Hamburg to a student, who since then has been sending me photos of my old sofa.

From the first of March I'll be a Polish entrepreneur. The company is called “Weber Software”. Kasia wanted it to be called “Klucz”. Maybe the next one.

---

Yesterday evening there was dinner at Mrs Danuta's. The first dinner at which I'm officially Kasia's boyfriend. Mrs Danuta roasted a duck with apples, made Silesian dumplings with gravy, red cabbage and lightly salted cucumbers. For dessert there was a gateau. A gateau! On an ordinary Friday.

— Mum, it's not a birthday — said Kasia.

— I know what it is — answered Mrs Danuta and kissed me three times.

Tomek came with Ewelina. Ewelina brought wine. Tomek brought an empty carrier bag because, as he said, he knew Mum would give him something to take home.

At dinner Grandma Tereska looked at me and Kasia and said loudly:

— Didn't I tell you?

— Grandma, at Christmas you said he was my fiancé. He's not my fiancé. He's my boyfriend.

— For now — said Grandma Tereska. And went back to her duck.

While we were washing up, Mrs Danuta stood next to me at the sink. Kasia had gone out onto the balcony with Ewelina.

— Jonas — said Mrs Danuta quietly. — She's had her heart broken once already. Three years ago. It took her a long time to get over it. For a long time she didn't smile. Since she met you, she's smiling again. — She handed me a plate to dry. — I'm not saying this to scare you. I'm saying it so you know.

— That boy left her a week before the wedding — she went on quietly. — He left her for a colleague from work. He threw away everything they had planned together. For a long time Kasia didn't trust anyone. She doesn't show her emotions, but she feels everything in a double dose.

— I know — I said. — I'll be careful.

— I know you will. — Mrs Danuta smiled. — But if you weren't, Tomek has a gun.

I think it was a joke. When Tomek heard it, he didn't say it was a joke.

One evening Kasia came for a lesson. I was looking at her instead of at my notebook.

— Has our programmer fallen in love, by any chance? — she asked.

— Maybe.

— This is a serious relationship, isn't it? You have to commit. And realise that in reality I'm awful in the mornings, I can't cook and I have a difficult personality.

— Your best quality is honesty.

— My best quality is instinct. From the first lesson I knew we'd be seeing each other on more than just Thursdays.

Kasia supports me a lot. We keep in touch every day, even when we don't see each other. We arrange lessons that then aren't lessons. Michael would probably say it's inappropriate. He'd probably be right. Sometimes I have a guilty conscience, but only for a moment.

---

In three weeks, on the first of March, we open the wall. They'll take off my plaster at the end of February. Mr Zbyszek is still sleeping in the cellar. The patrol drives past every hour. Everything is quiet.

Too quiet, says Auntie Halina. Zieliński hasn't done anything for three weeks. No letters, no phone calls, no roses.

— When a dog stops barking — said Auntie — it means it's getting ready to jump.
