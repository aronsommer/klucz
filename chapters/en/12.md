# Chapter 12. St Andrew's Eve

_Sunday, 29 November 2026, very late_

First, briefly, what happened over the last two weeks, because I haven't been writing. Kasia will be cross. I was busy. That's not an excuse, it's a fact.

Mr Lewandowski, the lawyer, received us in his flat in Kleparz. He is thin, almost bald, wears a bow tie and has a voice even the walls listen to. He read all the documents in silence, and then he said only one sentence:

— Without proof that Antoni Nowak never existed, we'll lose.

Then he added that the first hearing would be in March, that the company has three lawyers and we have him, and that this is a fair balance of power. Mr Henryk burst out laughing. The lawyer didn't.

Over the last two weeks the company tried to get into the cellar twice. The first time two men in helmets came with papers. Mr Zbyszek didn't let them in. The second time three men came without helmets and without papers. Mr Zbyszek didn't let them in either, but I called Tomek. Tomek arrived in a police car and the men drove off.

There was an article in the “Gazeta Krakowska”: “Tenants from Kazimierz fight for their home”. With a photo of Mrs Halina and Bonifacy. Mrs Halina bought ten copies.

The journalist did a long interview with Mrs Halina. In the article she wrote that Mr Zieliński's company has a partner: a foreign investment fund looking for profit on the Kraków property market. In an official statement the company answered that it cares about the good of the residents and about its reputation.

And for two weeks I worked like crazy, because Michael's project was running late. I finished on Friday at three in the morning.

But today is Andrzejki, St Andrew's Eve.

---

Andrzejki is the evening before St Andrew's Day, the thirtieth of November. Kasia explained to me that in the old days girls told their fortunes on this evening, whether and when they would get married, and now it's mainly an excuse for a party.

The party was at Magda's, Kasia's friend, in Podgórze. A big old flat, full of people, music and smoke from the kitchen. There were maybe thirty people. Most of them Kasia's age: teachers, IT people, a doctor, some actor, two students from Ukraine and a guy from Portugal who spoke Polish with an accent even worse than mine. I liked him at once.

At the party I realised that I don't know Polish. I know textbook Polish and Mrs Halina's Polish. The Polish of young people at a party is a different language.

— Hey, mate! “Ogarniasz”, what's going on? — a tall guy with a beard asked me.

— “Ogarniam”? — I repeated.

— Yeah, “czaisz”? You get it?

— I see. I “ogarniam” a bit.

— “Spoko”, chill. — He gave me a beer. — I don't “ogarniam” either. “Masakra”, how many people there are here.

Afterwards Kasia translated the whole conversation for me. “Ogarniać” means to understand, but also to organise, to sort out, to tidy up. “Ogarnij się” means “behave normally”. “Spoko” is “OK”. “Masakra” is something very bad or very good, it depends on the tone. “Serio?” is “really?”. And “ziomek” is a mate.

— And if I say “siema, ziomek” to Mrs Halina?

— Then she'll throw you out of the window. And she'll be right.

Then there were even more words. When Magda saw that someone had brought very cheap wine, she shouted: “Jesus, what is this shit?” The guy with the beard said about his boss: “A total arsehole, but he pays.” Someone was telling a joke about a woman at the doctor's. Someone else was telling gossip about an actor who had supposedly had an affair with the director's wife. In the kitchen someone was making drinks from vodka and apple juice and saying it was a traditional Polish drink. Kasia says that isn't true, but that alcohol in Poland always has some tradition or other.

The director supposedly knew nothing, although the actor's lover, that is his own wife, acted in all his films. Magda poured the rest of the cheap wine into the sink and said: “Shitty wine, shitty gossip.”

Two guys in the corner were arguing tenderly about which of them was better-looking. One of them, Paweł, told me later that they had been a couple for five years and that in Kraków being gay is much easier today than ten years ago. In the countryside it's a different story.

At about ten everyone was already a bit drunk. Not very, but enough to start singing. Rui sang a song in Portuguese, and then everyone together sang Polish songs which I didn't know, but everyone else knew every word. Kasia told me to relax and have fun.

— Hey, German! Move your “tyłek” and come and dance! — shouted Magda.

I had no choice.

Kasia told me later that “tyłek” is the more polite version of the word “dupa”, bum, and that you mustn't use either of them in front of Mrs Halina. On the dance floor Rui danced like a professional. I danced like Mr Zbyszek's fridge.

— Ladies first — I said to Magda at the door to the balcony, because that's what the textbook teaches. Magda said I was charming and old-fashioned, and that she was going to follow me on Instagram right away.

Then Magda gave me a lesson in Polish insults, “only the polite ones”: frajer, świr, palant, głupiec, drań, świnia, małpa. Sucker, nutter, prat, fool, scoundrel, pig, monkey. You can say any of them in front of your grandma, if you smile. Gęba, morda and łeb mean face and head, but rudely. And “dupsko” is a very big bum.

The guy with the beard was talking about his ex-girlfriend:

— She screwed up my whole year. She drove me mad from morning till night. It made me furious.

— So why were you with her? — asked Magda.

— Because she was gorgeous and sexy. Ha! Pathetic, eh?

— Bloody pathetic — Magda agreed. — Off to the kitchen with you and bring some ice. Bye!

The guy with the beard came back with the ice and said it wasn't his fault that he messed up the relationship. He just has bad luck.

— The devil knows if it's bad luck or character — said Magda. — Your bad luck talk always ends the same way. You play the tough guy, and then you cry in the kitchen.

Rui told jokes in Portuguese and nobody understood them, but everyone laughed. Someone was shouting at her boyfriend: “What on earth did you do?!” Magda said it was normal Polish entertainment.

Soon people were dancing like mad.

Then the mood became pleasant and romantic. Someone switched off the main light. Couples were dancing slowly. I was staring at Kasia and pretending I wasn't staring. Kasia was pretending she didn't see it.

When Magda flirted with me by the balcony, Kasia wasn't jealous at all. At least that's what she claimed. Magda told me afterwards in the kitchen that Kasia is never jealous, she just “observes very carefully”.

---

At eleven the fortune-telling began. Magda put a bowl of cold water, a candle and a pot of hot, liquid wax on the table.

— We pour the wax through the bow of a key, through this little hole at the top — she said. — Then we look at the shadow on the wall and say what we see. That's your future.

— Who's got a key? — someone asked. — An old one, with a big bow?

Everyone searched their pockets. Modern keys have too small a bow. Kasia looked at me.

I took out Grandma's key. For a moment it went quiet.

— Wow — said Magda. — That's a real key. May I?

They poured the wax through my key one after another. The wax fell into the water, went hard and took strange shapes. Then they held it by the candle and shadows appeared on the wall. Magda's was a dog (“You'll get a dog or a husband, it's almost the same thing”). The guy from Portugal got a plane (“You'll go back home”). One of the students got a heart. Everyone shouted and clapped.

— Guess what it is! — Magda called out each time. Everyone guessed, mostly wrong. Rui did a trick with a coin and a paper ball, like a magician. Magda's grandma, who was sitting in the corner, said: “In my day young ladies told fortunes to find a man. And now they do it for fun.” Then she looked at Kasia and added that she was a pretty girl.

Then it was Kasia's turn. She poured slowly, carefully. The wax made a long, twisted shape in the water. When she held it up to the candle, a shadow appeared on the wall.

— That's… — began Magda.

— A key — said someone.

It really was. The shadow looked like a key. A big, old key, just like mine.

Everyone looked from Kasia to me and back. Someone whistled. Kasia went red, for the first time since I've known her.

— It doesn't mean anything — she said quickly. — The wax went through a key, so it's shaped like a key. That's physics, not magic.

— Sure — said Magda. — Physics.

Then there was a second fortune, with shoes. The girls put their shoes down in turn, one behind the other, from the wall to the door. The one whose shoe touches the threshold first will be the first to get married. Kasia's shoe touched the threshold first. Kasia said it was because she has the biggest feet. Nobody believed her.

---

Later we danced. I can't dance. Kasia says I can't, but I try, and in Poland that counts. At about one we went out onto the balcony. It was cold and calm. Down below the last trams were running. There was fog over the Vistula.

— Kasia — I said.

— Hm?

— That shadow on the wall.

— Physics — she said. But she didn't walk away. She was standing very close. I could see her breath in the cold air.

And then my phone rang.

Mrs Halina. At one in the morning.

— Mr Jonas — she whispered. — There's someone in your flat. I can hear them through the wall. Several of them. They're walking around and throwing things. I'm scared.

— Lock your door with all the locks. Don't go out. I'm calling the police.

Kasia was already dialling Tomek's number.
