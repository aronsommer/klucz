# Chapter 21. The Lawyer

_Thursday, 25 February 2027_

Today they took my plaster off. My right leg is now thin, pale and very weak, as if it belonged to someone else. The doctor says it will be normal in a month if I do my exercises. Auntie Halina says it will be normal if I eat. At least I can walk without crutches now, slowly, with a walking stick. With the stick I look, as Kasia says, “like an English lord after a hunt”.

In the afternoon we went to Mr Lewandowski, the lawyer. Me, Auntie, Kasia and Tomek. The lawyer called in the morning and said he had “new facts that change the picture of the case”. The lawyer never says “I have good news”. He says “new facts”.

---

The lawyer's flat in Kleparz looks like a museum of law. There are thick books everywhere: codes, commentaries, case law, old volumes of “Państwo i Prawo”. On the desk the files lie in neat piles. On the wall hangs a portrait of his father, who was a lawyer too, and a diploma from nineteen sixty-four. It smells of dust, coffee and pipe tobacco.

The lawyer sat down at his desk, put on his glasses and opened a folder.

— Ladies and gentlemen. Let's start from the beginning, so that everyone understands what the dispute is about. — He looked at me. — Mr Weber especially.

He explained everything to us, slowly and precisely, as if at a lecture.

In proceedings for the return of a property there are parties. The applicant is Mr Zieliński's company, which bought the rights from the supposed heir. The other participants in the proceedings are the city, as the current owner of part of the building, and the owners of the flats that were bought, that is Auntie Halina and me. The company claims that Jan Nowak, the owner before the war, had two sons: Stanisław and Antoni. That Antoni was killed in forty-four in the Warsaw Uprising. That Antoni had a daughter, Jadwiga, and her only son is Edward Lis, born in fifty-six. And that in seventy-five Stanisław gave up his part in favour of his brother's family, so the whole of Jan Nowak's estate goes to Edward Lis.

— That's not true — said Auntie Halina. — Grandfather had one son. My dad. There was no Antoni.

— I believe you — said the lawyer. — But the court doesn't believe, the court checks. The company has presented documents. Antoni Nowak's baptism certificate from a parish whose archive burned during the war, so it can't be compared with the original. A copy of a death certificate from forty-four. Stanisław's declaration from seventy-five, with a signature. Statements from two witnesses who supposedly knew the family. All these documents look correct. No expert has examined them yet.

— And me? — asked Auntie. — I'm a living witness.

— You're a participant in the proceedings, not an impartial witness. The company's lawyers will say you have an interest in lying. — The lawyer took off his glasses. — We need a document. Preferably from those times. A will, a notarial deed, an entry in the land register from before the war. Something that can't be forged in five minutes on a computer.

— Maybe it's behind the wall — I said.

— Maybe. — The lawyer looked at me for a moment. — But that's not why I invited you. Mr Wiśniewski has the new facts.

---

Tomek put a photo on the desk. Black and white, a bit blurred, from the camera on our staircase. A young man in a hood, with his face turned to the side. But on his neck you could clearly see a tattoo. A snake.

— It was recorded a week ago, during the night from the seventeenth to the eighteenth — said Tomek. — He came into the gateway, checked the cellar door, saw the camera and ran off. But this time we have a face. We ran it through the system. — He put a second photo on the desk, in colour, from the police files. — Patryk Lis. Twenty-six. A record: assaults, thefts, one burglary and shooting a mate. He lives in Warsaw, in Praga.

— Lis — Kasia repeated slowly.

— Lis — said Tomek. — Edward Lis, the supposed heir, is his uncle.

We all looked at Auntie Halina. Auntie went very pale.

— Mum — she said quietly — was born Lis. Maria Lis. She had a brother, Kazimierz. Uncle Kazik lived in Warsaw. He married Auntie Jadzia, who was born Kowalczyk, not Nowak. They had two sons, Edek and Mirek. — She closed her eyes. — Edek Lis. He came to us for the holidays when he was little. He stole our sweets and killed frogs in the courtyard. Dad couldn't stand him.

— So he's your cousin — said the lawyer. His voice was very calm, but his eyes were as lively as a young man's. — A cousin on your mother's side. His mother was never the daughter of any Antoni Nowak. Mr Lis has no right at all to Jan Nowak's estate. But he knows the family history. He knows names, dates, addresses. He knows that Grandfather had a tenement. He knows that you and your sister didn't speak to each other for half a century and that neither of you would fight for the house. He knows enough to invent a brother who never existed.

— And Zieliński paid him for it — I said.

— Probably. The company paid Mr Lis two hundred thousand złoty for the rights to the estate. According to the valuer's estimate, that's less than ten per cent of the value of the building. People don't sell a real inheritance for ten per cent of its price. People sell something for ten per cent of its price when they know it's false.

---

The lawyer told us what he would do now. He would file a report with the prosecutor's office on suspicion of a crime: fraud and forgery of documents. He would apply to the court to appoint an expert to examine Antoni Nowak's baptism and death certificates. He would apply for Auntie Halina to be heard. And he would ask the court to move the hearing from March to April, “because new circumstances have come to light”.

— The civil case is about ownership — the lawyer explained. — The criminal case is about a crime. They're two different trials, two different courts, two different procedures. In the first case we have to prove something, in the second the prosecutor does. But one case can help the other a great deal. If the criminal trial ends with a verdict that the documents were false, the company will have no defence at all in the civil trial.

— The company will argue that everything was legal — the lawyer went on. — Their lawyers will demand that the court not admit new evidence, and they'll push for a quick judgment. We have to show that Antoni's documents are false. It's enough if the expert considers it likely. The rest is a matter for the prosecution in the criminal case. I must warn you, though: nothing is certain until the judge reads out the verdict.

— And Patryk Lis? — I asked.

— I'll deal with Patryk Lis — said Tomek. — We have his face on the recording, we have the fingerprints from the break-in in November. If they match, that'll be enough for an arrest warrant. And once we've arrested him, we'll see how much he knows about Zieliński. People like him are rarely loyal when they're facing prison.

— As for Patryk — Tomek added — the file already contains quite a lot. The prosecutor's office is passing us the CCTV recordings. The technicians took prints from the door handle and are now confirming whether they match. We've also found out that he uses several phone numbers. It's hard to say where he is now. Watching his mates' flats hasn't produced anything so far. I don't think I need to explain that this stays between us. I'll let you know as soon as anything happens.

— I've already talked to the chief — he added. — Our criminal department is working with Warsaw. We've confirmed Patryk's identity in three systems. Now we just have to find him.

— My people have been looking for him for a week. We tracked him down once, but he disappeared again. He probably has a hideout at his mates'. People like him commit petty crimes: holding up petrol stations, thefts, sometimes attacking someone in a bar. Once he shot a mate with a pistol and claimed the gun went off by itself. Nobody believes a trigger pulls itself.

— And Zieliński?

— Zieliński has bodyguards, a safe in his office and lawyers instead of a conscience. The company is just a cover for him. You don't catch people like that in the street. You catch people like that on paper.

— Careful — said the lawyer. — Mr Zieliński knows that too.

— This isn't an official's mistake — he added after a moment. — It's fraud. The company is knowingly misleading the court. The question is what's behind it. — He looked at Auntie. — And please don't blame yourself, Mrs Halina. It's not your fault that someone used your story.

Then the lawyer said we had to avoid hurrying, because any wrong step could get in our way. Tomek suggested that his colleagues would guard the tenement at night. The lawyer agreed it was necessary. Auntie said you could always rely on the lawyer.

As we were leaving, the lawyer stopped Auntie Halina at the door. He took her hand. Very old-fashioned, like in an old film.

— Halina — he said. It was the first time I had heard him call her by her first name. — Józek would have been proud of you.

— Józek would have said I'm a mad old woman.

— That too. — The lawyer smiled. — But proud.

---

In the evening Kasia and I sat in my kitchen. She was marking her students' essays. I was looking at the key.

In four days we open the wall. Mr Kaczmarek, the notary, has confirmed. The lawyer will come, Tomek, Mr Zbyszek with tools, a photographer from the “Gazeta Krakowska”. Auntie already knows what she'll cook. Mr Henryk already knows there'll be nothing there anyway.

— Are you afraid? — asked Kasia, without raising her head from the exercise books.

— Yes.

— Of what?

I thought for a long time.

— That it'll be empty — I said. — That after fifty years we'll find only bricks and dust behind the wall. And that Grandma… that she wanted to say something, and I won't get to hear it in time.

Kasia put down her pen. She came over to me and put her hands on my shoulders.

— Your grandma has already told you a lot. — She touched the small silver key round her neck. — Whatever is behind that wall is just the rest.
