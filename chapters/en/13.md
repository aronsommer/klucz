# Chapter 13. The Break-in

_Monday, 30 November 2026_

When we arrived by taxi, there were already two police cars in front of the tenement. Blue lights were reflected in the windows. The neighbours were standing on the pavement in coats thrown over their pyjamas: Mr Henryk, Mrs Grażyna, Ola with the baby in her arms. Mr Zbyszek was talking to a police officer and waving his arms.

Tomek came out of the gateway. He was in uniform and had a very serious face.

— They ran off across the courtyard before we arrived — he said. — Three or four of them. A car was waiting on the corner. Mrs Halina is unhurt, she's sitting at home with a colleague from the patrol. But your flat…

— What about my flat?

— See for yourself.

---

I didn't recognise my flat.

All the books were lying on the floor. Someone had thrown them off the shelves, opened them, torn the covers. The wardrobe was empty, Grandma's clothes were lying in a heap in the middle of the room. Drawers pulled out, buttons scattered everywhere. The mattress in the bedroom had been cut open with a knife. In the kitchen someone had opened all the cupboards and emptied flour, sugar and kasha onto the floor. They had even opened the fridge. My new, too-big fridge was standing open and quietly beeping.

My laptop was lying on the table. They hadn't taken it. They hadn't taken the money from the drawer or my grandfather's watch either.

— These weren't thieves — said Tomek. — Thieves take laptops. These were looking for something specific.

When I had calmed down a bit, I looked round the flat again. I looked closely at the wardrobe, the bed, the shelves. Only then did I realise how thorough they had been. They hadn't left a single place unsearched. As if they knew what to look for.

I knew what they were looking for. I had the key in my pocket. The whole evening, at the party, during the fortune-telling. If I had left it at home…

Then I noticed that something was missing.

— The album — I said. — The photo album. It was lying here, on the table.

It wasn't there. Not anywhere. We searched for half an hour. The album had disappeared. But I had the photo “Zosia and Hala” in my wallet. For a week I had always carried it with me.

— So they only stole the album — said Tomek. — I'll drop by tomorrow morning, we'll check if anything else is missing.

And one more thing.

— Where's Bonifacy?

Bonifacy had been at my place when I went out. He was sleeping on the bed. Now the door had been forced, the kitchen window was open, and the cat was gone.

---

The police worked until four in the morning. They took photos, measured, collected fingerprints with a special powder. Tomek's colleague, Sergeant Majewska, wrote a report. I had to sign it. I didn't understand everything, but I asked for a copy. I'm copying out a part of it:

> **Report on the inspection of the scene of the incident**
>
> On 30 November, between 1.40 and 3.55, an inspection was carried out in residential unit no. 4 at 7 Lipowa Street in Kraków in connection with a report of a break-in. Damage to the front door caused by forcing was found. Inside the unit disorder was found indicating that the rooms had been searched. The injured party, the German citizen Jonas Weber, stated that a photo album of sentimental value had been taken from the unit. No other losses have been found at present. Fingerprint traces were secured…

— “Sentimental value” — said Sergeant Majewska. — Nobody gets a sentence for that. But we can go after them for the break-in.

Then she talked to Mrs Halina. Mrs Halina was sitting in her kitchen, very pale, with tea she wasn't drinking.

— I saw one of them — she said. — Through the peephole. When they were leaving. Young, tall, in a hood. He had a tattoo on his neck. A snake.

— Would you recognise him?

— I recognise everyone I've seen once. — Mrs Halina raised her head. — I'm eighty, not ninety.

— Please calm down — said Sergeant Majewska gently. — Nobody will hurt you. We'll be nearby.

Sergeant Majewska wrote down: “snake on the neck”. Tomek looked at me. In the cellar, at the beginning of November, a man in a hood. Now again a man in a hood.

Sergeant Majewska explained to me what would happen now. The police will start an investigation. They'll compare the fingerprints with the database. If they manage to identify the perpetrator, the prosecutor can charge him and the court can punish him.

— Usually these aren't lone criminals — she said. — They work in groups. A gang like that has someone who gives the orders and someone who does the dirty work. The second kind we catch easily. The first kind almost never.

— And if you find him?

— Then we'll arrest him and question him. He'll have two options: keep quiet or talk. Most of them talk. Nobody likes sitting in a cell for someone else.

Before she left, she also took samples from the door handle and the cupboard. She said she would inform the prosecutor's office and that tomorrow I had to come to the “posterunek” to report the break-in officially and give a statement. Tomek says a real cop says “komisariat”, police station, not “posterunek”, but Sergeant Majewska is fifty and says what she likes.

---

I slept for three hours on the sofa at Mrs Halina's. She didn't sleep at all. When I woke up, breakfast was on the kitchen table: scrambled eggs, bread, tomatoes, coffee. And Mrs Halina, sitting by the window and looking at the courtyard.

— He's not here — she said. — Bonifacy is missing. I called him all night. As soon as it's light, I'll look in the cellar again.

— We'll find him.

— He's a stupid cat. — Her voice broke. — A stupid, fat, ginger cat. My husband brought him to me a year before he died. As a little kitten, in a hat.

It was the first time I had seen Mrs Halina cry. I didn't know what to do, so I put my hand on hers. She didn't pull it away.

---

At ten the phone rang. An unknown number.

— Mr Weber? Ryszard Zieliński. — His voice was soft and warm like a blanket. — I heard what happened last night. Terrible. Really terrible. Kraków isn't as safe as it used to be. I just wanted to say that I'm very sorry. And that my offer still stands. Perhaps now you'll look at it differently.

For a moment I couldn't speak. Then I said, very slowly, so that every word would be correct:

— I know who did it, Mr Zieliński.

Silence.

— Mr Weber. — The voice wasn't warm any more. — In Poland words like that can land you in court. I advise you to be careful. About yourself. And about your neighbours. Older people are so… fragile.

He hung up.

That wasn't a conversation. That was a warning.

His behaviour changed in one second. Every policeman knows behaviour like that, says Tomek.

My hands were still shaking for another five minutes. I wrote the whole conversation down in my notebook. Word for word. Tomek says it might come in useful.

Kasia said it was a threat. Tomek said it was a threat that can't be proved. Zieliński is too clever to say anything directly. “Older people are fragile” can mean everything or nothing. But I know what it meant. And he knows I know.

---

In the evening we cleaned up the flat. Everyone helped: Kasia, Mr Zbyszek, Ola, even Mr Henryk, who said the whole time that nothing will ever change in this country. Mr Zbyszek repaired the door and put in a new lock. While he was at it, he finally made the kitchen door too. He said it was “to cheer me up”.

Ola made a poster on the computer and hung it in the gateway: “Missing cat. Ginger, fat, answers to the name Bonifacy (sometimes).”

What made me angriest wasn't the break-in itself, but that someone had rummaged through Grandma's things: her dresses, her letters. Obviously they were looking for the key. When I asked Tomek if the burglars would come back, he said: “Nonsense.” But he didn't look convinced.

While cleaning up I cut my hand on some glass. A small cut, but it bled like hell.

At about nine I went down to the cellar for some planks. And there I found him.

Bonifacy was sitting in the dark, in the small space behind the brick arch. He was sitting in front of the younger wall and looking at it as if he were waiting for something. When he saw me, he said “miaow” in a very offended voice.

I carried him upstairs. Mrs Halina took him in her arms and said nothing for a long time.

— Where was he? — she asked at last.

— In the cellar. By the wall.

Mrs Halina closed her eyes.

— Zosia always said that cats know more than people — she said quietly.

Later Mr Zbyszek worked out how it had happened: the burglars had shut the cellar door, and Bonifacy was inside. He had been trapped there all day. Nobody had hurt him, but his pride had been hurt very badly.
