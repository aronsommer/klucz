# Chapter 16. The Truth

_Sunday, 27 December 2026_

Mrs Halina kept her word.

She knocked on my door today at ten in the morning. She was wearing a black dress, as if she were going to church or to a funeral. In her hands she was holding an envelope. That envelope.

— I know you saw it — she said. — It was lying under the rosary differently from before. I notice things like that.

— I'm sorry. I didn't open it.

— I know. I didn't open it either. For fifty-four years. — She sat down at the kitchen table, on Grandma's chair. — Make me some tea, Jonas. This will be a long story.

I made tea. Bonifacy lay down on her lap. Outside it was snowing. Mrs Halina talked for two hours. I didn't interrupt her. Now I'm writing down everything I remember, in her words, as exactly as I can.

---

“We were born in this house. Zosia in forty-two, during the war, me in forty-six, when the war was already over. Our grandfather, Jan Nowak, owned the tenement. He had a fabric shop downstairs, where the café is now. He was a rich man before the war. After the war the communists took everything from him. The house, the shop, the money. They left us two rooms on the second floor. Strangers moved into the other flats, people the state assigned. Grandfather died in fifty, of anger, as Mum used to say.

Dad, Stanisław, was an engineer. A quiet man. He hated the communists, but he never said so out loud. He used to say: ‘In this country, girls, the walls have ears.’ Mum was a music teacher. She played the piano and we sang. I always sang out of tune. Zosia said I sang out of tune so beautifully that it was a talent.

Zosia was four years older and better at everything. Cleverer, prettier, braver. I loved her more than anyone in the world. And I was a little jealous of her. Younger sisters are like that.

My beloved sister. She sacrificed a great deal for me: she carried me in her arms, she taught me to read, she defended me against the boys in the courtyard. And what did I do for her in return? I always thought she never betrayed anyone, that there was something holy in her. Now I know that her courage was a sacrifice too.

In seventy, in December, on the coast, the army shot at workers. Many people were killed. On television they said they were hooligans and bandits. Zosia was in her last year at university then. She came home after the news white with anger. At night she went out. In the morning “LIARS” was written in red paint on the university wall.

It was a crime. They shot people who only wanted bread at a normal price. They had raised food prices just before Christmas.

I knew it was her. She had paint on her hands.

In January they came for me. Not for her. For me. Two men in grey coats, from the Security Service. I was working at the university library then, I was twenty-four and I had a fiancé, Józek. They took me for questioning. I sat in a small room without a window for nine hours. They didn't beat me. They didn't need to. They spoke calmly. That they knew it was someone from our house. That they could take Dad's job away. That Józek wouldn't get a passport, wouldn't finish his studies, wouldn't find work anywhere. That Mum would lose her school. That everything depended on me. One name. Just one.

They made people talk in other ways: with the fear of losing their job, their flat, their future. They were capable of anything, and we knew it.

Fear destroys a person slowly. It takes away their senses, their reason, their courage. Only dust is left.

I was young and I was afraid. That isn't an excuse. It's the truth.

I told them.

They took Zosia the next day. They held her for three days before they let her go. They threw her out of university half a year before her degree. Dad lost his position at the design office and worked as an ordinary draughtsman until he retired. And I got what they had promised me: Józek got his passport, I stayed at the library. Everything was fine. Everything was fine for fifty years.

All my life I thought I was a traitor. Mum once said that ‘traitor’ is the worst word in the Polish language. Because I betrayed my sister, and betrayal in a family is the worst sin. My conscience didn't let me sleep. Anger at myself, shame, regret. Józek said: ‘Hala, it's not you who's guilty, it's the system.’ But the system has no face. I did.

For a long time I blamed them. Then myself. Then them again. But guilt can't be split in half. I lied to everyone: my parents, my friends, myself. For fifty years I didn't reveal it to anyone except Józek. The suffering I caused Zosia came back to me every night.

Zosia found out in March. I don't know from whom. Maybe from them. They liked it when people hated each other. She came to my room, stood in the doorway and only said: ‘Why, Hala?’ I couldn't answer. I still can't.

They had secret informers everywhere: at work, at the university, in the church. That was their method. Anyone could inform on you, so nobody trusted anybody. That was worse than fear.

In May she met Klaus. Your grandfather. He was an engineer from Hamburg, he came to Nowa Huta on a contract, he was installing some machines. Tall, calm, he spoke Polish even worse than you did at the beginning. She fell in love with him, or she wanted to fall in love. I don't know. In October they got married. In November she left. As the wife of a West German citizen, the authorities couldn't keep her.

Before she left, in October, she went down to the cellar with Dad. They were there all night. Through the floor I heard Dad working. In the morning Dad had mortar on his hands, and Zosia had a key on a red ribbon round her neck. They never told me what they had walled up there. Dad only said: ‘This is for both of you, when you stop being stupid.’ He died seven years later. We didn't stop.

In the photo you have, we're standing under the tree in June seventy-one. We're holding hands because Mum asked us to. She wrote on the back: ‘Together forever.’ Mum always believed in us.

In seventy-two that letter came. From Hamburg. I recognised the handwriting. I didn't open it. I was afraid there was hatred inside. I was even more afraid there was forgiveness inside. I didn't deserve that. I wrote on the envelope that the addressee refused to accept it, and I sent it back.

Then twenty years went by.

In ninety-five, when Poland was already free, Zosia came for the first time. She bought our old flat from the city, next to mine. She knocked on my door. She stood there, grey-haired, with her nose, with her eyes, holding that same letter in her hand. And the key. She said: ‘Hala, the letter is for you. The key too. When you're ready.’ I took them. I didn't say anything. I closed the door.

I gave her the key back after a year. I left it outside her door, in an envelope. Without a word. I kept the letter. I never opened it, but every evening I knew where it was.

You're probably thinking: why didn't I apologise to her? Why didn't I make up with her? An apology seemed too small to me. What was I supposed to say? ‘Sorry I ruined your life’? There are no words for harm like that. I thought it was my fate: to live behind a wall and never admit to anything.

That thought became my obsession: that if I opened the letter, my whole world would fall apart.

And then for almost thirty years Zosia came every summer. For two months. She lived behind the wall. I heard her radio, her steps, her cough. We passed each other on the stairs. We said ‘good morning’ to each other. Like neighbours. Thirty years of ‘good morning’.

In August there was a phone call from the hospital in Hamburg. That she was dead. That her will said I should be informed. That was all.

At the cemetery I lit four candles. For Dad. For Mum. For Zosia. And one for that stupid girl from the library who in January nineteen seventy-one was more afraid than she was loving.

Now you know. You can hate me. You have the right.”

---

For a long time I didn't say anything. Outside it was already noon. The snow had stopped.

I thought about Grandma. About how in Hamburg she sat by the window every evening. About how every Christmas she laid one place too many at the table and said it was a Polish custom, for a traveller. About the note in the dictionary. Four words: I love, I miss, I'm sorry, I forgive.

— I don't hate you — I said at last. — And I don't think she hated you.

— How can you know?

— Because I'm here. — I took the key from the table. — Grandma could have left this flat to anyone. She could have sold it. She left it to me. Together with the key. And she wrote that you should be informed. I think she knew I would come here and knock on your door. I think that was her plan.

Mrs Halina looked at the envelope in her hands.

— I'm not ready to read it yet — she said. — But maybe soon I will be.

— And the wall in the cellar?

— Dad said it was for both of us. — She smiled weakly. — Zosia is gone. But you're here. Give me a little time, Jonas. I waited fifty years. A few more weeks won't kill me.

Then she stood up and did something I didn't expect. She kissed me on the forehead.

— And call me “ciocia” — she said. — In Polish: ciocia, auntie. Your grandmother's sister is an auntie too. It's simpler that way.
