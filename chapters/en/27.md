# Chapter 27. The Court

_Tuesday, 20 April 2027_

Courtroom number one hundred and fourteen at the District Court for Kraków-Śródmieście is small, bright and very ordinary. I expected something like in American films: wood, marble, tall windows, a public gallery, journalists. Instead: white walls, plastic chairs, a long table for the judges, two smaller tables for the parties, a stand for the witnesses, a monitor on the wall and the Polish emblem, a white eagle with a crown on a red background.

But there was a public. Everyone came. Mrs Danuta closed the bar and put up a sign: “Closed for reasons of justice”. Mr Zbyszek came with bandages on his hands, which weren't really needed any more, but looked very good. Ola and Bartek came with little Zosia in a baby carrier. The Nowaks sat in the front row, so as to hear everything. Magda, Rui, Ewelina. The journalist from the “Gazeta Krakowska”. And a dozen or so people I didn't know: neighbours from other tenements in Kazimierz, from which Zieliński had also wanted to throw people out.

On the board in front of the courtroom hung the cause list, that is the list of today's cases: divorces, a car theft, a rape, debts, inheritances. Kasia said that in a court you can see the whole life of a city.

On the other side of the room, at the applicant's table, sat the company's three lawyers. Expensive suits, leather briefcases, laptops. Mr Zieliński wasn't there. For ten days he has been in pre-trial detention. The court decided he would stay there for three months, because there is a risk he would obstruct the proceedings.

On our side sat Mr Lewandowski. Alone. In an old gown, with one thin folder.

— Three against one — Mr Henryk whispered to Mrs Grażyna.

— A fair balance of power — Mrs Grażyna whispered back. She remembered.

---

— All rise, the court is coming in.

The judge came in. A woman, maybe fifty, in a black gown with a purple jabot and a gold chain with an eagle round her neck. She sat down, adjusted her glasses, opened the files and looked round the room so that everyone immediately fell silent. Even little Zosia.

— I open the session in the case brought by the company Zieliński Development with the participation of… — she read out a long case number and a long list of participants. My name was on the list. It sounded strange, read out in Polish: “Jonas Weber, citizen of the Federal Republic of Germany”.

The hearing lasted five hours. I didn't understand everything. Court language is even harder than office language: “I move that the application be dismissed”, “I ask for an objection to be entered in the record”, “I disallow the question”, “I order a break”, “documentary evidence”, “an undisputed circumstance”. Kasia sat next to me and whispered translations from Polish into Polish.

I'll describe the most important parts.

Before testifying, every witness makes a pledge. In a Polish court you don't swear on the Bible, but on your conscience. The judge reads out the formula and the witness repeats it: “Aware of the meaning of my words and of my responsibility before the law, I solemnly promise that I will tell the honest truth, concealing nothing that is known to me.” Kasia says it's the most beautiful sentence in Polish law.

— It's not an oath, it's a pledge — she explained to me in a whisper. — Oaths are in America, in films.

---

**The expert.** First the document examiner gave evidence. An older man with a beard, with a sheet full of numbers. He had examined Antoni Nowak's baptism and death certificates. He said the paper really was old, from before the war. But the ink wasn't. The ink on both documents was toner from a modern laser printer, and the stamp had been made in a computer program. “With great care,” he added, “but without any knowledge of history. The parish stamp uses a typeface that was created in nineteen eighty-two.” Stanisław Nowak's declaration from seventy-five was also false: the signature had been copied from another document, and the typewriter it was written on didn't yet exist in Poland at the time.

Then he had examined Jan Nowak's will from forty-nine. The ink, the paper, the handwriting compared with other documents of Jan Nowak's from the city archive. “An authentic document,” said the expert. “I have no doubts whatsoever.”

One of the company's lawyers stood up and began asking questions. Was the expert sure? Could it be ruled out that the document from the cellar was a later copy? Did the expert know in what conditions the document had been kept? The expert answered calmly, briefly and each time the same way: “Yes.” “No.” “I know, because it's in the notary's report.” The lawyer sat down.

The company's second lawyer also suggested that someone might have planted the document in the cavity recently, for example after the fire. The expert just smiled and showed a photo of the mortar: fifty years of dust can't be faked.

— Really, Your Honour — said Mr Lewandowski — the claim that someone planted the document after the fire is absurd.

---

**Edward Lis.** He gave evidence by video link, from the hospital in Warsaw. On the monitor he looked even worse than in his flat. He was wearing hospital pyjamas and had an oxygen tube under his nose. He spoke slowly, but clearly. He said everything he had told us. About the debts, about Zieliński, about the law office in Mokotów, about how they invented Antoni.

The company's lawyer asked whether Mr Lis was taking strong painkillers and whether that might affect his memory.

— I take morphine — said Edward Lis. — But morphine didn't give me two hundred thousand złoty. Your client did.

Someone in the room laughed. The judge looked up and it was quiet again.

---

**Halina Kowalska.** Auntie gave evidence last. She walked slowly to the witness stand, in a black dress, with her handbag in her hand. The judge asked for her first name, surname, age, address and relationship to the parties. Auntie answered very clearly, as if in a lesson. Then the judge warned her about the criminal liability for giving false evidence.

In Poland false testimony carries a sentence of up to eight years in prison. The judge said it very calmly, like a weather report.

— I know how one statement can destroy a life, Your Honour — said Auntie. — I once gave one like that. In January nineteen seventy-one. Not in a court, but in a room without a window. It was true, but it destroyed my sister's life. That's why today I will say only the truth.

And she told them. Everything. About Grandfather Jan, about her dad Stanisław, about Zosia. About December seventy and the writing on the wall. About the interrogation. About how she said one name. About fifty years of silence and thirty years of “good morning” on the stairs. About the key, the wall, the letter from her dad.

The room was so quiet that I could hear Mr Zbyszek breathing. Mrs Danuta was crying. The journalist from the newspaper stopped writing.

At the end Auntie said:

— Your Honour, there was no Antoni. My grandfather had one son. My dad had two daughters. One is dead. The other is standing before the court and is very ashamed that she needed fifty-six years to say it out loud.

The company's lawyers didn't ask her a single question. The court briefly heard both representatives once more.

A just verdict, I thought, isn't only a legal question. It's a question of truth.

---

The judge ordered a break. An hour. Then she came back and read out the decision.

— The court assesses the evidence, not the people — the lawyer had told me during the break.

I don't remember all the words. I remember the most important ones:

“The court dismisses the application in its entirety… The court finds that the estate of Jan Nowak was inherited in its entirety by his only son, Stanisław Nowak… The estate of Stanisław Nowak was inherited by his daughters, Zofia Weber, née Nowak, and Halina Kowalska, née Nowak, in equal halves… The court notifies the prosecutor's office of the suspicion that a crime was committed by persons acting on behalf of the applicant…”

The decision isn't final yet. The company can appeal. The lawyer says it won't appeal, because the company no longer has a chairman, only a prisoner. And that an appeal in a case like this would be “procedural suicide”.

In giving reasons, the judge said that the court had to weigh all the evidence and address each piece of it. That facts can't be ignored just because they're inconvenient. She pointed out that the documents presented by the company had been forged, the company had broken the law, and the applicant's actions were contrary to the principles of honesty.

When the judge left, the room was quiet for a second. And then Mr Zbyszek stood up and started clapping. Then Mrs Danuta. Then everyone. Mr Henryk clapped the loudest and shouted: “Justice!” The court clerk said you mustn't clap in a courtroom. Nobody listened to her. She was smiling herself.

Mr Lewandowski slowly closed his thin folder. He took off his glasses. He wiped them with a handkerchief. He looked at Auntie Halina.

— Well, there you are — was all he said. — A fair balance of power.

---

In the evening there was a party at the “Pod Aniołem” bar. Mrs Danuta opened the bar specially for us. There were as many pierogi as anyone wanted, and as much vodka as anyone could take. Mr Lewandowski drank three glasses and told a story about a case from nineteen eighty in which he defended workers from Nowa Huta, and how the judge fell asleep during his closing speech. Grandma Tereska danced with Mr Zbyszek. Tomek proposed to Ewelina. On his knees, in front of everyone, with a ring in a kompot glass. Ewelina said “yes” and lifted him off his knees with one hand.

Auntie Halina was sitting in the corner, by the window, with Bonifacy, whom Mrs Danuta had allowed her to bring “just today”. I sat down next to her.

— Auntie, what will we do with the tenement now? It's ours now. Yours and mine.

Auntie looked at all the people in the bar. At Ola and Bartek, who were dancing with Zosia in their arms. At Mr Henryk and Mrs Grażyna, who were arguing about whether to dance. At Mr Zbyszek.

— Nothing — she said. — We won't do anything. People will stay where they are. The rent will stay what it was. We'll renovate the cellar. The roof too. Mrs Wanda will come back if she wants to, Zieliński's flat on the first floor will have to go to someone anyway. — She smiled. — Dad wrote: “Houses come and go.” But this house will stay a little longer.

Then she took an envelope out of her handbag. The one marked “For Hala”. Still sealed.

— I'll read this letter in Gdańsk — she said. — All her life Zosia wanted to go there with me. To the coast. To those workers because of whom she wrote “liars” on the wall. Will you come with me in the summer?

— I will.

— And bring Kasia. — Auntie gave me a meaningful look. — And bring a ring. Tomek will show you how it's done. Just not in a kompot glass.
