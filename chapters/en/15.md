# Chapter 15. Christmas Eve

_Thursday, 24 December 2026, at night_

Mrs Halina came back from hospital on the eighteenth. She has new medicines, a new diet (no salt, no fat, no cheesecake, in other words, as she says, “no life”) and she's not allowed to climb the stairs with shopping. I do the shopping now. The list is always very precise: “Butter, but not the one in gold paper. Bread from Mrs Basia, but not yesterday's. Sour cream eighteen per cent, not twelve, not thirty.”

Mrs Danuta invited us both to Wigilia, the Christmas Eve supper. At first Mrs Halina said no, because she always spends Wigilia alone, with Bonifacy and the radio. Then Mrs Danuta came to see her in person, with a poppy seed cake. They talked for two hours. I don't know what about. When Mrs Danuta was leaving, Mrs Halina said: “Well then, see you on Thursday.”

---

Kasia prepared me for Wigilia as if for an exam.

— First we wait for the first star. When it appears in the sky, we sit down at the table. Before that you mustn't eat anything. Nothing. Even if Tomek cries.

— All right.

— There's hay under the tablecloth. There's one extra place at the table, for a lost traveller. Anyone who knocks on Christmas Eve must be let in.

— And if Zieliński knocks?

— Then he'll get a carp on the head. Next. Before supper we share the opłatek, the Christmas wafer. Everyone with everyone. You take the wafer, go up to a person, give them your wishes, they break off a piece of your wafer, you break off a piece of theirs, and then you kiss on the cheeks. Three times.

— Everyone with everyone?

— Everyone with everyone. With Uncle Marian too.

— Who's Uncle Marian?

— You'll see.

---

Mrs Danuta's flat is above the bar. When we came in, it smelled of mushrooms, fried fish, cabbage and cake. There was a white tablecloth on the table, and on it more food than I had ever seen in one place.

I counted. Twelve dishes, as tradition says:

1. red barszcz with uszka (uszka are small pierogi with mushrooms),
2. mushroom soup,
3. fried carp,
4. fish Greek style,
5. herring in oil with onion,
6. herring in sour cream,
7. pierogi with cabbage and mushrooms,
8. cabbage with peas,
9. kutia (wheat with poppy seeds, honey and nuts),
10. dried fruit kompot,
11. poppy seed cake,
12. gingerbread.

All without meat. On Christmas Eve you don't eat meat. Instead you eat so much that afterwards you can't move.

There must have been a ton of food on the table. Mrs Danuta says nobody may leave her Wigilia hungry and that hunger is the biggest crime in her house. In the kitchen she had supplies for a week: a basket of pierogi, a big pot of bigos, loads of biscuits and extras for everything. For thirty years she has been feeding the whole family, and sometimes, as Tomek says, half of Kraków.

The hay under the tablecloth is a reminder that Jesus was born in a stable. The empty place reminds us of those who aren't with us. Kasia says that Wigilia brings all Poles together, even those who argue the whole year round. And that some people put a carp scale under their plate so they'll have money all year. I put two. Just in case.

There were nine of us at the table. Mrs Danuta, Kasia, Tomek, Mrs Halina, me. Uncle Marian, Mrs Danuta's brother, with his wife, Aunt Bożena. Their daughter Zuzia, who is eight and asks questions non-stop. Grandma Tereska, Mrs Danuta's mother, who is ninety-one and only hears what she wants to. And the empty place for the traveller.

Everyone came in their best clothes. Tomek in a jumper with a reindeer, which he got a year ago from Grandma Tereska and which he hates.

From four o'clock Zuzia stood at the window looking for the star. At four forty she shouted: “Daddy, there it is! There it is!” Tomek said it was a plane. Zuzia said it was the star. Grandma Tereska said it was Venus. In the end we decided that would do.

Kasia told me that in Poland Christmas is celebrated above all on Christmas Eve, in the evening. The twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth are days for the family, for visits and for eating what's left over. “The main thing is that there's a lot and that it's from the heart,” says Mrs Danuta.

---

Then came the opłatek. That was the hardest moment of the evening, because you have to make the wishes yourself, off the top of your head, and different ones for each person. Kasia taught me a few phrases. Everyone uses them:

“Health, happiness, prosperity.”
“May all your dreams come true.”
“All the best, lots of joy and peace.”
“May things go well for all of us.”

I wished Mrs Danuta that the bar would always be full and Zieliński always far away. She laughed and kissed me three times, very hard.

Uncle Marian gave me wishes that lasted three minutes and were about politics, Germany, the European Union, petrol prices and the Polish national football team. At the end he said: “But you're a decent lad,” and gave me a glass of vodka, although at Mrs Danuta's you don't drink on Christmas Eve. Mrs Danuta pretended not to see.

Kasia explained to me afterwards that Uncle Marian has had the same opinion about everything for thirty years: idiots rule in Brussels, idiots rule in Warsaw, and prices are going up. But he's the best uncle in the world, because he's always the first to come when someone needs help.

Grandma Tereska looked at me and said: “Kasia's fiancé is handsome.” Kasia said: “Grandma, he's not my fiancé.” Grandma Tereska said: “Not yet.” And then she pretended not to hear.

Then Kasia stood in front of me. She was holding the wafer in both hands.

— I wish you — she said quietly — that you find what you're looking for.

— I already have the key — I answered.

— I'm not talking about the key.

She kissed me on the cheek. Once. Not three times. Zuzia noticed and said so out loud. Everyone laughed.

---

Mrs Halina shared the wafer with me last. She held my piece in her fingers for a long time.

— You know — she said, and for the first time she said “ty” to me, not “pan” — on Christmas Eve I always gave Zosia the biggest piece of the wafer. Because she was older. My sister always got everything first. And I…

She stopped talking. I saw in her eyes that she had heard what she had said. It suddenly went quiet at the table. Even Zuzia was silent.

— My sister — Mrs Halina repeated, very calmly. As if she were trying out how the word tasted after fifty years. — Yes. — She looked at me. — Not today, Jonas. Please. Today is Christmas Eve. After Christmas. I promise. I'll tell you everything.

— All right — I said. — After Christmas.

She broke off a piece of my wafer. I broke off a piece of hers. She kissed me three times, and her cheeks were wet.

Then Grandma Tereska started singing a carol. “Wśród nocnej ciszy głos się rozchodzi”, “In the silence of the night a voice spreads”. After a moment everyone was singing. I didn't know the words, so I just listened. Mrs Halina sang the loudest and, as usual, a bit out of tune.

The carols are beautiful and very old. In one the angels sing “Glory in the highest”. In another the shepherds bring gifts for the Christ Child. Grandma Tereska said that Mrs Danuta's barszcz was divine. Mrs Danuta said it was Grandma Tereska's recipe. Grandma Tereska said she had never in her life given her that recipe.

Then Zuzia performed a little poem about the star, which told the whole Christmas story in eight lines. After her performance she got five złoty and a kiss from Uncle Marian.

---

Then there were the presents under the tree. Mrs Danuta cried over the chopping board. Tomek ate half the gingerbread police car in five minutes. Mrs Halina said the slippers were “too pretty to walk in” and put them on straight away.

Kasia opened my little parcel last. She looked at the small silver key for a long time.

— Physics — she said at last. And she put it on round her neck.

I got two things from her. A book, a thick dictionary of idioms, with a dedication: _“For Jonas, so that he knows what he's saying when he says he knows nothing. K.”_ And a wooden frame. Just right for one old photo.

At midnight everyone went to midnight mass at the church. Me too. The church was full, cold and beautiful, people were singing, and I stood next to Kasia and thought that for the first time since Grandma's death I didn't feel alone.
