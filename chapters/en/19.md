# Chapter 19. Ward Number Six

_Wednesday, 20 January 2027_

This is my fifth day in hospital. The doctors wanted to let me out on Monday, but it turned out I had a high temperature and something with my lungs, so I'm staying until Friday. Kasia and Tomek had to go back to Kraków: she to her students, he to work. Kasia calls three times a day. Tomek sends photos of food.

There are four people on ward number six. Mr Staszek, a bus driver with a bad stomach, who complains about the hospital food the whole time. A young guy from Warsaw, Kuba, a snowboarder with a broken collarbone, who plays games on his phone the whole time. Me. And Mr Władysław.

Mr Władysław is eighty-seven and has a broken hip. He's a highlander from Zakopane. For forty years he was a Tatra mountain guide and a TOPR rescuer. When he heard how I broke my leg, he laughed for a long time, and then he said:

— Son, in the mountains there are two kinds of people. Those who are afraid, and those who don't know yet that they should be afraid. You've just changed groups.

Since then we've been talking all day long. He talks, I listen. It's the best Polish lesson I've ever had. Apart from the lessons with Kasia, of course. (Kasia, if you're reading this: of course.)

---

Mr Władysław was born in thirty-nine, three months before the war. His father was a Tatra courier. During the occupation he led people across the mountains to Hungary: soldiers, politicians, Jews fleeing from death. He also carried letters, money and documents for the government in exile. In winter, at night, over passes that are hard to cross even in summer.

— The Germans caught him in forty-three — said Mr Władysław. He looked at me. — Not your Germans. Those Germans. That was a different story.

— What happened to him?

— Auschwitz. He didn't come back. — Mr Władysław was silent for a moment. — Mum said that to the end he believed Poland would be free. He was right, only a bit too early. Forty-six years too early.

— First they tortured him in Zakopane, in the “Palace”, the Gestapo torture chamber — he added. — He said nothing.

He also talked about the Jews from Kraków, from Kazimierz, my district, whom the Germans shut up in a ghetto on the other side of the Vistula and then deported to the camps. His father led two Jewish families across the mountains.

— People talk about heroes — he said. — But he was simply afraid. Every day. But he did what had to be done. Courage isn't the absence of fear. Courage is fear that keeps going.

— The commander of their group was a major from Kraków — he added. — Father always called him: my comrade.

— The Germans wanted to make us a nation of slaves — he went on. — The resistance was the only answer. The British air force sometimes dropped weapons in the mountains, but rarely. They sent political prisoners to the camps. Father said violence breeds violence. But sometimes a man has no other way out.

— They held public executions in the streets. They said they were the master race. And then they lost and suddenly none of them knew anything.

Mr Władysław says that the three of us, him, Kuba and me, are like three comrades from the army. Mr Staszek doesn't belong to our three, because he snores. When Mr Władysław talked about his father, it moved me very much. I felt something strange: pride, although it wasn't my father.

— Every human being deserves respect — he said. — Even someone who snores.

He talked about communist times, when tourists from all over the country came to Zakopane on works holidays, and the highlanders sold them oscypki and moonshine under the counter. About how he smuggled jeans and coffee across the border with Czechoslovakia. About how in seventy-nine in Nowy Targ he stood in the crowd when the Polish pope came, and how hundreds of thousands of people sang together.

— That was the first time I felt there were fewer of them than of us — he said. — The communists. That we were bigger. A year later there was Solidarity.

About his wife, who died two years ago and with whom he danced at every wedding in the whole area. About his son, who lives in Chicago, and about his grandchildren, who speak Polish “like Americans”. About how he rescued people in the mountains: tourists in trainers on Orla Perć, skiers under avalanches, once even a cow.

— A cow?

— The cow fell into a gully. The head shepherd cried like a child. We pulled her out on ropes for six hours. She survived. — Mr Władysław smiled. — The most grateful patient of my life. For ten years she gave the best milk in all of Kościelisko.

Mr Władysław also told me that his brother had a kidney transplant at a clinic in Kraków, in eighty-nine. His son gave him the organ.

— The tissues matched perfectly — he said. — The doctors said it was a miracle. My brother said it was just a good family. And the highlanders say that the old gods live in the Tatras, older than all the churches, and that they're the ones who decide who survives.

---

Life in hospital has its rhythm. At six the nurses take your temperature and blood pressure. On the first day they also measured my height and weighed me, as if I were in the army. At seven there's breakfast: bread, butter, cheese or jam, tea without sugar. Mr Staszek says this tea is a crime. At nine there's the ward round: the doctors go from bed to bed, read the charts, ask questions and talk to each other in Latin. Then physiotherapy. The physiotherapist is teaching me to walk on crutches. At first it was hard. Now I can get to the bathroom and back in three minutes. Mr Władysław says it's the record for ward number six.

Mr Staszek has gastritis and something with his liver. The doctors are doing tests on him: blood, ultrasound, various tests. Mr Staszek says his liver is healthy, because he only drinks at weekends and only beer. The doctor says the liver doesn't know the days of the week. Kuba had an operation on Monday. The surgeon put a metal plate into his bone. Kuba is very proud of it and shows everyone his scar. He says that as soon as he's out, he'll go back to the slopes, because without sport his body goes mad. His club even has a snowboarding section for children.

Sister Marta, the senior nurse, is the most important person on the ward. More important than the doctors. Everyone is afraid of her, even the head of the ward. But when someone can't sleep at night, Sister Marta comes, sits on the bed and talks until they fall asleep. She came to me once. We talked about Germany. Her son works in Munich as a lorry driver.

— I miss him — she said. — But he won't earn as much here. All the young people are leaving. And you came. You're a strange one.

Mr Staszek keeps butting in. When Mr Władysław talks about the war, Mr Staszek talks about his bus. When Kuba talks about snowboarding, Mr Staszek says that in his day there were only sledges and that was enough. At night he snores so that nobody sleeps well. But when Kuba was sad because his girlfriend didn't come, Mr Staszek gave him his chocolate and said: “Don't be so unhappy, son. Girls come and go. Chocolate stays.”

Sister Marta treats all patients the same: the head of the ward, who was once in here with his appendix, and the homeless man they brought in at night from the station. She says that in hospital everyone is equal, because everyone is wearing the same pyjamas.

---

This morning, during the ward round, a delivery came. A courier brought a huge bunch of white roses. The nurses were delighted. Kuba said: “Somebody loves you.” Mr Staszek said: “Or somebody wants to kill you. White flowers are for funerals.”

There was a card with the bouquet. Nice paper, elegant handwriting.

> _Mr Weber, I wish you a speedy recovery. The mountains are dangerous. The city can be dangerous too. Please take care of yourself. R. Z._

My hands were shaking. How does he know I'm here?

Mr Władysław took the card from me and read it twice.

— Who is this?

I told him everything. About the tenement, the wall, the key, the break-in. About Auntie Halina. He listened in silence, with his eyes closed. When I had finished, he didn't say anything for a long time.

— My father — he said at last — used to say that there are people who want to frighten you because they can't beat you. If someone sends you roses with a threat, it means they're afraid. People who aren't afraid don't write cards.

Then he called Sister Marta and asked her to take the roses out of the ward.

— It's a shame about the flowers — said Sister Marta.

— The flowers aren't to blame for anything — said Mr Władysław. — But let them stand somewhere else. In the corridor.

---

In the afternoon Auntie Halina called. Her voice was nervous.

— Jonas, last night someone tried to get into the cellar again. Mr Zbyszek chased them off. He shouted so loud that he woke the whole tenement. But listen. The lawyer says we can open the wall without you, on Monday, as we planned.

— Then open it. The main thing is that it's quick.

— No. — Auntie was firm. — I won't open it without you. Zosia gave the key to you, not to me. I gave my key back, remember? I've moved it to the first of March. Until then Mr Zbyszek will sleep in the cellar, and Tomek has promised a patrol will drive past every hour.

— Auntie, it's dangerous.

— Waiting fifty years was dangerous. Five weeks is nothing.

She hung up before I could protest. It runs in the family, as Kasia says.

Mr Władysław heard the whole conversation. Now he's lying there looking at the ceiling.

— Stubborn women — he said. — The best kind of women. My wife was stubborn too. For forty-nine years I argued with her and I never won once.

— Do you regret it?

— I only regret that there wasn't more of it. Of those arguments. — He turned his head to the window. Through the window you could see Giewont, pink in the setting sun. — Sleep, son. Tomorrow will be better. In the mountains tomorrow is always better.

Before I fell asleep, Mr Władysław also said:

— In life you have to know how to let go. Whoever can't let go will go mad. When I was young I wanted to control everything. Then the mountains taught me that there are some things you have no influence on: the weather, an avalanche, a woman. You can only take care of those you love and not waste time on anger. Evil will finish itself off. And revenge isn't a solution. It's just another problem.
