# Chapter 5. The Milk Bar

_Saturday, 17 October 2026_

On Saturday morning I got a message from Kasia:

> _Hi! Have you got plans for lunch? My mum runs a milk bar on Starowiślna. The best pierogi in Kraków. It's also a Polish lesson, because Mum talks non-stop :)_

I wrote back that I had no plans. It's true. In Kraków I don't have any plans yet, only a key, a cat that isn't mine, and a fridge too big for the kitchen.

We agreed on two o'clock.

---

A milk bar is a very Polish thing. Kasia explained to me that once, in communist times, these were cheap canteens for workers and students. Milk, cheese, eggs, flour, potatoes, in other words cheap food without meat. Now there is meat too, but the prices are still low, and the food is like at your grandma's.

Kasia also explained to me that in Poland people don't eat lunch at noon, but “obiad”, at two or three o'clock. Obiad is the most important meal of the day: soup, a main course, kompot. Supper is small. Breakfast depends on the person.

There are lots of restaurants in Kraków: Italian, French, Chinese, Japanese, even Georgian. But Kasia says nothing can replace a milk bar. Here nobody pretends. You eat fast, cheaply and well.

The bar was called “Pod Aniołem”, “Under the Angel”. Inside it was bright, loud and full of people: students with laptops, older men with newspapers, mothers with children, two workers in dirty clothes. The menu hung on the wall. I copied it into my notebook:

> **Soups:** żurek with egg, red barszcz, tomato soup with rice, chicken broth with noodles
> **Main courses:** pierogi ruskie, pierogi with meat, pierogi with cabbage and mushrooms, pork cutlet with potatoes and salad, gołąbki in tomato sauce, potato pancakes, pancakes with cheese
> **Drinks:** kompot, tea, coffee, apple juice

Behind the counter stood a woman who looked like Kasia thirty years from now. The same green eyes, the same smile, only her hair was lighter and her voice twice as loud.

— Is that him? — she asked Kasia before I could say “good afternoon”. — That German?

— Mum, this is Jonas. Jonas, this is my mum, Mrs Danuta.

— Good afternoon, Mrs Danuta. Nice to meet you.

— Nice to meet you too, me too! How polite! And how thin! — Mrs Danuta looked at me like a doctor at a patient. — What do you eat? You don't eat anything. Sit down, I'll bring something right away.

— But we haven't ordered yet… — Kasia began.

— I know what you need.

After five minutes there were two plates of żurek on the table, twelve pierogi ruskie, twelve with meat, a cutlet, a salad, potato pancakes and two big mugs of kompot.

— Mum, this is food for ten people!

— He'll eat it, he'll eat it. He's young.

I ate. Not everything, but a lot. The żurek was sour and hot, with white sausage and egg. Pierogi ruskie have potatoes, cheese and onion inside. I didn't know why they're called that. Kasia explained that it's from Rus, the old lands in the east, and not from Russia.

---

When there were fewer people, Mrs Danuta sat down with us.

— It's wonderful that you came. It's much better here than in a restaurant, isn't it? — she said. — During lunch I didn't want to disturb you, but now I have to have a word with you, son.

— Well then, tell me. Where are you from, what do you do, how much do you earn, do you have a wife?

— Mum!

— What? I'm asking. Normal questions.

— I'm from Hamburg — I said. — I'm a programmer. I don't have a wife. I earn… enough.

— He doesn't have a wife — repeated Mrs Danuta and looked at Kasia. Kasia looked at the ceiling.

— And a girlfriend? A partner?

— Mum, is this an interrogation or lunch?

— It's care — said Mrs Danuta. — Every mother wants to know who her daughter brings home.

— I didn't bring him home. I invited him to lunch.

— On a date — Mrs Danuta corrected her. — In my day that was called a date. Love starts with pierogi. Remember that, son.

Kasia hid her face in her hands. I laughed so much that I almost choked on my żurek.

— My mother used to say: whoever doesn't respect food doesn't respect people — added Mrs Danuta. — There's no arguing about taste, but everybody likes the pierogi they call ruskie. Next time I'll also recommend you the pancakes.

Then Mrs Danuta asked about Grandma. I told her everything: about the flat, the letter, the key, Mrs Halina and Zieliński. When I said “Zieliński”, Mrs Danuta stopped smiling.

— That man — she said quietly. — Five years ago he wanted to buy my bar. I didn't sell. Then suddenly there was an inspection from the health department. Then a second one. Then someone smashed my window. The police found nothing.

— Each time it was a coincidence. That's what they told me.

— Because there was no evidence — said someone behind me. It was Tomek. He was wearing a tracksuit, not a uniform. He sat down and immediately took a pieróg from my plate. — Zieliński is clever. He never does anything himself. He has people.

— Tomek, what are you doing? — said Mrs Danuta. — You turn up without a word and straight away stick your fingers in someone else's plate!

— In return I'll wash the dishes — said Tomek with his mouth full.

Mrs Danuta said she had been hearing that for thirty years.

— And hurry up, because your shift starts soon — she added.

— These things happen — said Kasia. — But not three times a month.

— What are you getting at? — asked Tomek.

— That someone planned it. Every detail. A man like that uses every weakness: fear, debts, loneliness. He used the fact that Mrs Wanda was alone.

— People like that should be put in prison — said Mrs Danuta.

— They've already put away many like that — answered Tomek. — But they always get out.

— What kind of people does Zieliński have? — I asked.

— All kinds. Lawyers, officials, friends. And a few lads who aren't lawyers. — Tomek ate a second pieróg. — Listen, Jonas. Show me that letter.

I showed him. Tomek read the one sentence several times.

— Concentrate — said Kasia. — And stop eating.

— “Klucz otwiera to, co zamknęłam” — he said. — “Zamknęłam”. Not “zamknąłem”. So a woman. So your grandma locked something herself.

— I know that — I said.

— But she didn't write “zamykałam”, she wrote “zamknęłam” — added Kasia. — That's the perfective aspect. Once, one single time, and finished. Something specific. Not “I always used to lock the door”, but “I locked something and it's still locked today”.

— My sister — said Tomek proudly. — A teacher. She has grammar for every problem.

— And you have a pieróg for every problem — answered Kasia.

— OK, OK. But seriously: if Grandma hid something, where? People always hide important things at home. Under the floor, in the wall, behind a picture. I've seen it in every operation I've taken part in.

— Operation? — I asked.

— Police operation. Searches. Don't ask, it's boring.

---

After lunch Mrs Danuta gave me a jar of bigos and said I had to come next Saturday. It wasn't a question.

— And drop in whenever you like — she added. — No need to call. Our door is always open, though Kasia says it's dangerous.

Kasia walked me home. We walked along the Vistula. It was cold, but sunny. Swans were swimming on the river.

Kasia told me about Kraków. That the city was made by merchants and kings, and the dragon from Wawel is only a legend. That Kraków was the capital of Poland for several hundred years, until the king moved to Warsaw, and that people from Kraków still think it was a mistake. Kraków lives in the past, says Kasia, and it's happy with that.

— Is it true that a lot of foreigners live in Kraków? — I asked.

— Quite a lot. Students, tourists, IT people. But you're the only one who came because of a key.

— Your mum is… — I searched for the word.

— Loud? Scary? Wonderful?

— Wonderful.

— Good answer. — Kasia smiled. — Listen, Jonas. If your grandma hid something in that house, and Zieliński is buying that house… then maybe he's looking for something too?

I hadn't thought of that. Now I can't stop thinking about it.
