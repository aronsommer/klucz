# Chapter 8. All Saints' Day

_Sunday, 1 November 2026_

For the whole week I tried to talk to Mrs Halina about the photo. On Monday I knocked on her door, in a scarf and a hat, just as she had told me. I showed her the photo. She looked at it for a long time, and then she only said:

— We were friends. The best. We called each other “sisters”. That's what you say when you're young.

— And what happened then?

— Then Zosia left. People leave. — She gave me back the photo. — Keep it somewhere safe.

She didn't want to say any more. Not on Tuesday, Wednesday or Thursday either. On Friday she didn't open the door, although I could hear she was at home.

Yesterday evening she suddenly knocked on my door.

— Tomorrow is All Saints' Day — she said. — I'm going to the cemetery. You're coming with me. I can't carry all those candles on my own any more.

It wasn't a question. In Poland a lot of things aren't questions.

---

On the first of November the whole of Poland goes to the cemeteries. Kasia had explained it to me before, but I didn't believe it until I saw it. The trams were full of people with flowers. In front of the cemetery there were hundreds of stalls with grave candles, chrysanthemums, wreaths, obwarzanki and sweets. Police officers were directing the traffic. It was cold, foggy and strangely solemn.

On the radio they said that more than a hundred thousand people would come to Rakowicki Cemetery alone today. Every member of the family has their job here: someone buys the candles, someone washes the graves, someone makes sure nobody forgets the grandma on the father's side.

Rakowicki Cemetery is huge. It's like a city: streets, avenues, old trees, stone angels, tombs as big as small houses. Mrs Halina walked fast, as if she knew the way by heart. I carried two bags of candles and a bunch of white chrysanthemums.

First we went to her husband's grave.

_“Józef Kowalski, of blessed memory, 1944–2009. Beloved husband.”_

Mrs Halina wiped the stone with a handkerchief, put down the flowers, lit two candles. Then she stood and prayed quietly. I stood next to her and didn't know what to do with my hands.

— He was a good man — she said at last. — Boring, but good. We lived together for thirty-six years and he never once raised his voice at me. Not every woman can say that.

— Did you have children?

— No. The Lord didn't give us any. — She looked at me. — Let's go on.

Then we went to the old part of the cemetery. Here the graves were grey, covered with moss, some of them sunken a little. Mrs Halina stopped at one of them. There was a black iron heart and a cross on it.

_“The Nowak family. Stanisław Nowak, 1912–1978. Maria Nowak, née Lis, 1918–1990. Rest in peace.”_

Mrs Halina knelt down and lit four candles. One red, two white and one green.

Before her wedding Grandma's name was Nowak. Zofia Nowak. I had seen it on her documents.

— Who were they? — I asked very calmly.

— Friends. Neighbours.

— My grandma's name was Nowak.

Mrs Halina stood up. She brushed off her knees for a long time.

— There are a million Nowaks in Poland, Mr Jonas — she said. — It's as if someone in Germany were called Müller.

That's true. And it isn't an answer.

— Why four candles? — I asked as well. — There are only two people in the grave.

Mrs Halina didn't answer. She took my arm and we went to the exit.

---

After dark the cemetery turned into a sea of lights. Thousands, maybe hundreds of thousands of candles were burning on the graves. Red, yellow, white. People walked slowly between the graves and talked in whispers. Someone was singing. It smelled of wax, wet leaves and smoke.

The candles burn all night. By morning, Mrs Halina says, they will all have burned out. When I first saw the whole cemetery in their light, I was lost for words. Mrs Halina says it's a sign of remembrance. A very simple act, but a dignified one.

I had never seen anything so beautiful and so sad at the same time.

Mrs Halina said that people don't come here only because of faith. Even those who don't believe in God light candles.

— The body is here — she said. — And the soul, well, nobody knows that. But memories are real. Only they remain. Mum used to say: “Do good, and good will come back to you.” We come so that the dead know we haven't forgotten them. Or so that we ourselves don't forget them.

On the walk to the exit she told me who was lying where: actors, poets, professors, generals, her friend's mother, the priest who married her. She knew the story of almost every grave. She talked about those people as if they were neighbours from our street.

— That's fate — she said. — We'll all end up here one day. Me sooner, you later. There's nothing to be afraid of. Eternal rest, as the prayer says. Besides, here at least nobody puts up the rent.

Kasia called when we were on our way back.

— Are you at the cemetery? Mum and I are at Salwator, with Dad. I mean, at his grave. Will you come to us for supper?

I didn't know that her dad was dead. She had never talked about him.

In the evening at Mrs Danuta's we ate chicken soup and talked about people who are no longer here. Mrs Danuta talked about her husband, about how he danced, how badly he cooked and how beautifully he whistled. Tomek talked about his grandfather, who was a miner in Silesia. I talked about Grandma.

Tomek's grandfather worked in a mine for forty years. Tomek says his grandfather always used to say: underground a man learns respect. For the darkness, for his workmates and for God.

It was a good evening. We laughed a lot. I suppose that's how it is in Poland: to remember the dead, you have to laugh together.

---

I came home after eleven. It was dark on the stairs, because the light bulb wasn't working again. While I was looking for my keys, I noticed that the cellar door was open.

The cellar door is never open. Mr Zbyszek always locks it with a padlock.

Now the padlock was lying on the floor. Broken.
