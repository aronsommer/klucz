# Chapter 7. Zosia and Hala

_Sunday, 25 October 2026_

A week in bed. I don't have a fever any more, and almost no cough either. Doctor Nowicka says I can go out on Monday. Mrs Halina says I can go out on Monday only in a hat and a scarf. I know who I'll listen to.

During this week Kasia came every day. She said they were lessons, and we really did study, but mostly we talked. About everything. About her students: about the Japanese man who speaks Polish better than she does, and about the American woman who for two years has only said “dzień dobry” and “piwo”. About my life in Hamburg. About why I don't have a girlfriend. (Kasia asked. I asked why she doesn't have a boyfriend. She said it was none of my business. That's not fair.)

On Wednesday she taught me the future tense.

— There are two — she said. — If you do something for a long time or often, you say: “będę robił”, “będę pisać”, “będę czekać”. If you do something once and to the end: “zrobię”, “napiszę”, “zjem”. For example: tomorrow I'll be lying in bed all day, but in the evening I'll eat up all of Mrs Halina's chicken soup.

— And if I lie and eat at the same time?

— Then you'll have soup in bed. I don't recommend it.

In the evenings, when Kasia left, I watched films. Kasia said it was the best way to learn: films with Polish subtitles. I was still too weak for Polish films, so I watched American films and series with Polish subtitles and noted down words. After a week I had a very strange dictionary in my notebook.

From a pirate film: captain, ship, deck, crew, island, treasure, sword, map. The captain shouted: “All hands on deck!”, and the crew ran. In the end they found treasure on the island: a chest full of gold. But the captain was sad anyway, because he had lost the love of his life.

From a crime series: detective, agent, investigation, killer, victim, murder, corpse, lead, suspect, alibi. The detective always said: “We've got a body.” The FBI agent always said: “This is not your case, detective.” After three episodes I knew who the killer was. Always the one who was nicest in the first episode.

From a war film: army, general, colonel, lieutenant, soldier, order, enemy, attack, front, base. “That's an order, lieutenant!” “Yes, sir, colonel!” The enemy attacked the base at night. Everyone was killed except the main hero, who was wounded but handsome.

From “Star Wars” and other science fiction films: planet, space, universe, rocket, satellite, spaceship, mission, mankind, laboratory, scientist, experiment, energy, technology. The captain's mission was to save mankind from the aliens. Mankind wasn't grateful to him.

From a vampire film: vampire, demon, monster, ghost, hell, moon, curse, witch, spell. At full moon the vampire craved blood, the demon wanted someone's soul, and the witch cast spells. I went to sleep with the light on.

From a children's cartoon: king, queen, prince, princess, kingdom, dragon, magic sword. The prince saved the princess from the dragon. The dragon was the most interesting character in the whole film.

From a western: sheriff, horse, wagon, desert, bandit, shot. The sheriff aimed at the bandit and shouted: “Hands up!”

From a spy film: spy, female agent, agency, department, headquarters, badge, poison, hostage. The beautiful agent had poison in her ring, a spy from an enemy agency kidnapped a hostage, and the head of the department shouted at headquarters: “Show me your badges!”

From a historical film: empire, ruler, slave, warrior, tribe, people, temple, gates. The cruel ruler of the empire ordered the temple gates to be closed, and the warriors of a wild tribe wanted to free their people. In the end the slave became king.

From a horror film: beast, skull, corpse, bloody, dark, deadly, serial killer. A body was found in a dark forest. The serial killer left a human skull next to each victim. He murdered six people before anyone thought of calling the police. The beast from the forest turned out to be innocent.

From a film about war in space: commander, fleet, warship, command. “We're losing the ship!” shouted the commander. “Hold your fire!” answered fleet command. Then there was a big shoot-out and all the bad guys were killed.

Kasia read my notebook and laughed for a long time.

— You know the word “spaceship”, but you don't know the word “fork”.

— What do I need a fork for, if I have a sword?

The next day she brought me an old Polish comedy from nineteen eighty-four, “Seksmisja”, about two men who wake up in the future, in a world without men. I understood maybe ten per cent. But I laughed, because Kasia laughed so loudly that Bonifacy ran away to the kitchen.

The title is a mix of two words: “seks” and “misja”, sex and mission. Kasia says that in eighty-four the film was doubly funny, because officially there was no sex under communism.

---

When I was alone, I went through Grandma's things. It wasn't easy. Every thing was like a small voice.

Her clothes were hanging in the wardrobe. Two dresses, a dark coat, a grey jumper that still smelled of her perfume. At the bottom there were shoes: black ones with heels, brown ones for walking, slippers. In the drawers there were handkerchiefs, gloves, scarves and a box of buttons. Grandma never threw anything away. All the buttons from a whole life.

In another drawer there were old cassettes, vinyl records and a reel of tape for a tape recorder. On one cassette someone had written in pen: “Hala sings, 1969”. I had nothing to play it on.

But wait a moment. This doesn't make sense. Grandma lived in Hamburg for over fifty years, and I lived with her almost my whole life. Why are her clothes hanging in a wardrobe in Kraków?

I called the notary. It turned out that Grandma came here every year, in summer, for two months. She never told me. I thought she went to a spa by the Baltic. That's what she told me.

On the shelves there were several hundred books: Mickiewicz, Sienkiewicz, Szymborska, Miłosz, crime novels, cookbooks, dictionaries, art albums, “The Master and Margarita” in Polish and an old chess textbook. In one dictionary, German–Polish, I found a note with my name. Grandma had written on it the words she wanted to teach me: “kocham”, “tęsknię”, “przepraszam”, “wybaczam”. I love, I miss, I'm sorry, I forgive. That's all. Four words.

I sat with that note for a very long time.

I remembered my childhood. I grew up in a small house near Hamburg. Grandma actually brought me up, because Dad was ill, and Mummy, as I called her then, worked all day. Grandma sewed toys for me: a teddy bear, a doll for my cousin, a little dragon made of green fabric. She never mentioned where she knew such patterns from. Now I know: from Kraków.

---

On Friday I found a photo album. It was heavy, in a leather cover, and very worn. On the first pages there were photos from before the war: an elegant man with a moustache, a woman in a hat, the same tenement house, only cleaner, with flowers in the windows. Then photos from the fifties: two little girls in the same dresses. Then from the sixties: two teenagers on bikes, two girls by the river, two young women at a ball.

Always two.

In the last photo there was Grandma, the same girl as on the wall, and the other one, the younger one. They were standing in the courtyard, under the tree, holding hands. I took the photo out of the album. On the back someone had written in blue ink:

_“Zosia and Hala. Lipowa 7, June 1971. Together forever.”_

Hala. In Poland that's the short form of the name Halina.

In the evening I scanned all the photos and saved them to a disk. Just in case.

---

On Saturday evening I called Mum. In America it was only two in the afternoon. Mum was in the garden, she had a hat on her head and a glass of wine in her hand.

— Jonas! How are you feeling? Is it cold there in Poland?

— Mum, did Grandma have a sister?

Mum was silent for a moment.

— I don't know. She never said. You know what she was like. She didn't say anything about Poland. When I asked, she changed the subject.

— Nothing? Never?

— Once. — Mum put down her glass. — When Grandpa died. She was very calm at the funeral, and in the evening she cried in the kitchen. She said: “I left someone in Kraków whom I will never forgive. And someone who will never forgive me.” I don't know if it was one person or two. She didn't want to say any more.

— I think she suffered her whole life — Mum added. — Only nobody saw it. She treated it as her private secret.

— Take care of yourself, Jonas — she also said. — It worries me a bit that you're there alone.

After the call I put the photo on the table, next to the key.

Behind the wall, in the flat next door, Mrs Halina turned on the radio. It was playing some old song. I could hear her singing along with the radio, quietly and a bit out of tune.

Together forever.

Tomorrow I can go out. I know whose door I'll knock on first.
