# Chapter 9. The Cellar

_Monday, 2 November 2026_

Last night I did something stupid. I went down to the cellar alone.

I know, I know. In every film the one who goes down to the cellar alone at night dies first. But I was tired, a bit tipsy from Mrs Danuta's vodka, and very curious.

I turned on the torch on my phone. The stairs were steep and narrow. Downstairs it was cold and damp, it smelled of earth, old wood and potatoes. A long corridor, and on both sides wooden doors to the storerooms: every flat has its own. On some doors there were numbers, on others names written in chalk.

The cellar was completely dark. My phone had no signal. I thought maybe I should go back. I didn't go back.

With every step I lost courage. The corridor got narrower and narrower. The smell of damp made my head spin. I thought of films in which someone discovers something terrible in a cellar. Once again I thought of going back. Once again I didn't.

It stank of mould. The ceiling was low, I had to bend down. At one point something gave me a fright: a cat? a rat? I didn't want to check.

I walked slowly. My steps were very loud. At one point I heard a sound. Metal on stone. Once. A second time. Somewhere at the end of the corridor.

— Hello? — I said. My voice was strangely high. — Is anyone here?

Silence. Then quick steps. Someone was running in the dark, not towards me, but the other way. A door slammed. I ran after him, but the cellar has a second exit, to the courtyard. When I got there, I only saw the back of a man in a dark hoodie. He jumped over the wall and disappeared.

I stood in the courtyard under the tree and my heart was pounding like a hammer.

I was terrified, but also a bit proud. A coward, supposedly, and yet I went down. Then for an hour I couldn't fall asleep. Every noise sounded like footsteps to me. When I finally fell asleep, I had a nightmare: I was running through a long tunnel under Kraków, and at the end Grandma was standing and saying: “Too fast, Jonas. Too fast.”

---

In the morning I told Mr Zbyszek everything. Before I had finished, he was already in the cellar with a big torch and a hammer. Just in case, I suppose. After a while Kasia came too, because I had called her. She brought coffee in a thermos and said she only had an hour, because she had a student at ten.

— He ran away this way — I said and pointed at the door to the courtyard. — He must have got out through this exit, and then over the wall.

— From the outside you can't open this door without a key, but from the inside you can — said Mr Zbyszek. — So he came in at the front, through the padlock, and went out this way.

In daylight, or rather in the light of Mr Zbyszek's torch, the cellar was less scary. It was simply old. Cobwebs, dust, old bikes, crates, jars of gherkins and jam. Mrs Halina had about a hundred jars in her storeroom. Each one had a label with a date.

In the corner there was an old bathtub, next to it a sledge and a child's pram from the seventies. Something ran across the floor.

— A rat — said Mr Zbyszek calmly. — Don't be afraid. It's more afraid than you are.

Kasia didn't look convinced.

At the end of the corridor, in the place that had looked like a dead end at night, there was a part of the cellar I didn't know. A low arch of red brick, and behind it a small, empty space. The wall was old and grey. But in one place, at the bottom, there were a dozen or so bricks of a different colour. Darker. As if someone had once made a hole in the wall and then bricked it up.

Someone had tried to open that hole in the night. There were fresh marks between the bricks, as if someone had hit them with a hammer or a chisel. There was red dust on the floor.

— Well, well — said Mr Zbyszek. — This wall is younger than the rest. This brick is from the sixties or seventies. See? A different colour, different mortar. And this here — he touched the old wall — this is still from before the First World War.

— Hold the torch — said Mr Zbyszek. — And shine it here, into the gap. — He looked for a long time. — I can guess who did this. But guessing isn't the same as knowing.

An old chisel was still stuck in the wall in one place. Mr Zbyszek carefully pulled it out.

— He wanted to break down the wall, but he didn't know how — he said. — The damage is small. It's an inside wall. If he'd hammered like that all night, he'd only have made a lot of noise. — He pointed at the back entrance to the cellar, from the courtyard. — He went out that way, just as you say. I've asked so many times for the lock there to be changed. Now I'll warn everyone again.

— What's behind this wall? — asked Kasia.

— How should I know? — Mr Zbyszek shrugged. — I've worked here for thirty years and I never paid any attention to it. It's dark here. Who would look at bricks?

I took out the key. There was no door or lock in the wall. But I had to try. I held the key to every gap, to every hole. Nothing. Of course nothing.

— The key opens what I locked — said Kasia quietly. — Maybe not a door. Maybe something behind a door.

— Or behind a wall — I said.

Mr Zbyszek raised the hammer.

— Well then? Shall we open it?

Then we heard a voice behind us.

— Don't touch that wall.

Mrs Halina was standing in the doorway. In a dressing gown, in slippers, with Bonifacy in her arms. We hadn't heard her come down the stairs.

— Mrs Halina… — began Mr Zbyszek.

— I said so. Don't touch it. It's not your cellar and it's not your business. — Her voice was calm, but her hands were shaking. — Mr Zbyszek, put on a new padlock. Two would be best.

She turned round and went upstairs. Bonifacy looked at us over her shoulder.

---

Mr Zbyszek put on two new padlocks. Tomek came at noon, looked at the marks, took photos and said what we already knew.

— Someone is looking for something. Without evidence I can't do anything. Nobody stole anything, nobody was beaten up. A broken padlock isn't a crime anyone goes to prison for.

— And if it's Zieliński's people?

— It's definitely Zieliński's people. — Tomek sighed. — But “definitely” isn't evidence. The court wants facts.

— The cellar is a shared area — he added. — Every tenant has the right to come in here, but no stranger. If we catch a stranger inside, that's a real case. Until then, don't come down here alone. Promise?

— I promise — I said. We both knew it wasn't true.

When everyone had gone, I sat for a long time in the kitchen without a door and looked at the key. Mrs Halina knows what's behind that wall. Zieliński probably knows too, or at least he guesses. Only I don't know anything. And it's my grandma's flat, my family, my story.

I wrote three questions in my notebook:

1. What's behind the wall?
2. Who was Mrs Halina to Grandma?
3. What does Zieliński want?

Kasia says that in Polish “kim” is the instrumental of “kto”. In the lesson she taught me that after “być” we use the instrumental: jestem programistą, ona jest nauczycielką, pani Halina była przyjaciółką babci. Or siostrą: her sister.

That I don't know yet.
