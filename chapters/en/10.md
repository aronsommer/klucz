# Chapter 10. The Office

_Thursday, 5 November 2026_

Today I got to know Polish bureaucracy. I'm as tired as if I had run a marathon.

It all started when Kasia asked if I was registered.

— You're an EU citizen, so you can live here as long as you want — she explained. — But if you live in Poland for longer than three months, you have to register your stay at the voivodeship office. You also need a PESEL number, without it you can't get anything done. Not the bank, not the doctor, not a phone.

— What's PESEL?

— A number. Every Pole has one. Eleven digits. It's like your second name, only more important.

---

I went to the office at eight in the morning. I thought I'd be first. I was thirty-seventh. A crowd of people was waiting in the hall: Ukrainians, Indians, two Spaniards, an older man from Vietnam and a very nervous Italian woman with a small child.

At the entrance there was a ticket machine. You had to choose your matter: “Residence permit”, “Residence card”, “EU citizens”, “Collecting documents”, “Information”. I chose “EU citizens”. I got ticket B37. The screen showed B12.

All the seats were taken. A board said: “Urgent matters: window 7”. Everyone had urgent matters. The older man from Vietnam had come at a very early hour, at six, and he was first. He told me he had been filling in these forms for twenty years and had never yet managed to fill one in without a mistake.

The screen also said that the current average waiting time was a hundred and twenty minutes. Next to it hung a poster: “Use our services online!” Kasia says the office website only works at night and on Sundays. The woman next to me said she had come for the third time, because twice she had been late and lost her appointment. The official responsible for this assures everyone it won't happen again. He has been assuring them for five years.

I waited two hours. In that time I learnt a lot of words, because I read all the posters on the walls: “application”, “attachment”, “form”, “declaration”, “stamp duty”, “signature of the applicant”. Kasia says it's a language nobody speaks except officials. Even officials only speak it at work.

When I finally saw B37, I went to window number four. A middle-aged woman was sitting there, in a flowery jumper and with very long nails.

— Yes, please?

— Good morning. I'd like to register my stay.

— Passport or ID card.

I gave her my ID card. She looked at it for a long time.

— Do you have the application?

— What application?

The woman sighed as if she had heard the question a thousand times. She probably had. She gave me a form.

— Please fill it in. In capital letters. Here first name, here surname, here date and place of birth, here your address in Poland. Here the purpose of your stay. Here your signature. You also need proof of health insurance and a document showing that you have the means to support yourself. Or an employment contract.

— I work for a company in Germany.

— Then please bring a certificate from your employer. Translated.

— Translated by whom?

— By a sworn translator.

— And I can't do it today?

— Today you can submit the application without the attachments. But then you'll get a request to supply what's missing. You have seven days for that. If you don't supply it, the application will not be considered.

— And you get the PESEL number at the city office, not here. That's a different procedure. And please don't make a mistake on the form, because every correction means a new queue.

— With today's application you'll receive the right certificate in a week. The regulations don't allow it any earlier.

— And the certificate from my employer?

— That needs the manager's signature. The manager is on holiday, and his deputy can't sign it. According to the instructions all documents must meet the standard, and we have to keep to those standards. Please come on Monday.

I understood maybe half. But I understood that I have to come back.

On the door next to it there was a notice: “Department of Civil Affairs. Applications are accepted from 8.00 to 15.00. In accordance with the regulations, documents must be delivered in the original. The obligation to hold a PESEL number applies to persons staying in Poland for longer than…”. I read it three times. Kasia told me later that nobody in Poland talks like that, except officials and one law professor she knew at university.

When I was already getting up, the woman suddenly smiled.

— Your Polish is very nice. You speak better than half the people who come here. And half of them are Poles.

That was the best moment of the day.

---

After the office I went to the notary. Mr Kaczmarek, the notary, has his office in an old house on the Main Square. There are diplomas on the walls, hundreds of folders in the cupboards, and Mr Kaczmarek himself looks as if he were made partly of paper too.

We talked about the inheritance. It turned out that everything is simpler and at the same time more complicated than I thought.

— What's simple — he said — is that the flat now belongs to you. Your grandmother bought it from the city in ninety-five. The will is valid. We did the certificate of inheritance in September. The flat is entered in the land register in your name. Nobody can take it away from you.

— And what's complicated?

Mr Kaczmarek took off his glasses.

— The house is complicated. Before the war the whole tenement belonged to the Nowak family. After the war the state took it over. The residents lived there as tenants and paid rent to the city. In the nineties some of them bought their flats, like Mrs Zofia and Mrs Kowalska. The rest still belongs to the city.

— I understand.

— And three years ago a man appeared who said he was the only heir of Jan Nowak, the owner before the war. He showed documents: that Jan Nowak had a second son, Antoni, and that he is Antoni's grandson. And a declaration from seventy-five in which Stanisław Nowak gives up his part of the house in favour of his brother's family. He applied for the tenement to be returned. And then… he sold his rights to a certain company.

I didn't need to ask which one.

— Mr Zieliński's company — said Mr Kaczmarek. — The case is in court. If the court decides that this man was the real heir, the company will get everything the tenants didn't buy: the courtyard, the cellars, the attic, the roof and the city flats. Then it will be able to raise the rent, do a renovation and… do what it likes with the people.

— I must point out, though — the notary added — that I'm not a lawyer. I can only set out the facts for you.

— And if he wasn't the real heir?

— Then that has to be proved. — Mr Kaczmarek smiled sadly. — And documents from before the war have often burned, been lost or never existed.

— Because of the age of the documents, we have to search in different sources — he added. — In the city archive, in church archives, in old registers. We have to collect everything connected with the Nowak family and put it into one application to the right court. But the balance of power is such that the company has time and money.

Jan Nowak. Stanisław Nowak from the grave at the cemetery was probably his son. And Zofia Nowak, my grandma, was Stanisław's daughter.

— Mr Kaczmarek — I asked — did Stanisław Nowak have any other children?

Mr Kaczmarek opened a folder and searched for a long time.

— According to the documents we have, he had one daughter. Zofia. — He closed the folder. — But those documents were prepared by the representative of Mr Zieliński's company.

---

In the evening Kasia came with cake from her mum and a folder for my documents from the office. She bought it specially. It's red and says “OFFICIAL MATTERS — DO NOT TOUCH”.

I told her everything. When I had finished, she was silent for a long time.

— If Mrs Halina is your grandma's sister — she said at last — then she's Stanisław's younger daughter. She knows whether Grandpa Jan had a second son and whether her dad ever gave anything up. And if there was no Antoni, then that man isn't an heir at all.

— And if we prove it…

— Then Zieliński's whole plan falls apart. — Kasia looked at me. — Now do you understand why someone is banging a hammer on the wall in your cellar at night?

---

Later Kasia corrected my diary. With a red pen, like in a school exercise book. There were quite a lot of mistakes, but, as she said, practically all of them were obvious ones.

— Anyway, this isn't a class register to be marked — she added. — It's your diary. I'm only giving you hints.

Indeed, her notes in the margin were short: “gender!”, “aspect!”, “right”, “nice”, “completely meaningless, but funny”. At the end she wrote: “Passed. Extremely fast progress. However: commas.” Naturally I was as proud as a child. Before that only one person had checked my texts: Michael, and only my code.
