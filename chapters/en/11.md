# Chapter 11. The Meeting

_Thursday, 12 November 2026_

On Monday all the tenants got a letter. The same one, in a white envelope, with the company logo: a golden house and the words “ZIELIŃSKI DEVELOPMENT”. I'm copying it out here, because it's good practice. And because I want to remember what it looked like.

> Kraków, 6 November
>
> **Dear Sir or Madam,**
>
> we kindly inform you that in connection with the ongoing proceedings for the return of the property at 7 Lipowa Street, our company, as the acquirer of the rights and claims, plans in the near future to carry out a comprehensive technical inspection of the building, including the cellars, the attic and the electrical and gas installations.
>
> We also inform you that after the proceedings have been concluded, a modernisation of the building is planned. The costs of the modernisation may affect the level of the charges paid by the tenants.
>
> We ask that you allow access to the premises and the cellar rooms within the set period, from 16 to 20 November, between 8.00 and 16.00.
>
> Persons interested in selling their premises on favourable terms are invited to contact our office.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> Ryszard Zieliński, Chairman of the Board

Kasia read it three times and said it was a masterpiece. There is a threat in every sentence, but not a single bad word.

— “May affect the level of the charges” — she read. — That means: you'll pay three times as much. “On favourable terms” — that means: run while we're still giving you money. And “a technical inspection of the cellars”…

— That means: we want to break down the wall — I said.

---

Mrs Halina called a meeting. At her place, on Thursday at six. She baked a cheesecake and made sandwiches, because, as she said, “nobody thinks on an empty stomach”.

Everyone who was still left in the tenement came.

Mr Zbyszek, in a clean shirt, which I saw for the first time.

The Nowaks from the ground floor: Mr Henryk, a retired railwayman who had an opinion on every subject, and Mrs Grażyna, who had the opposite opinion to her husband's on every subject. (They aren't my grandma's family. Mrs Halina was right: there are a million Nowaks in Poland.)

Ola and Bartek from the fourth floor, a young married couple with a baby. They live in a city flat and they're the most afraid, because if the house goes to the company, they could lose everything.

Kasia, who doesn't live in the tenement, but said she was “language support”. And me.

Bonifacy sat on the wardrobe and looked down at everyone, like a chairman.

---

The meeting lasted three hours. I'll only describe the most important moments, because I didn't remember everything. Everyone talked at the same time.

Mr Henryk: — I'll tell you how it is. This is capitalism. Whoever has money is right. Under communism at least you knew who was stealing!

Mrs Grażyna: — Henryk, don't talk nonsense. Under communism everybody stole, only quietly.

Mr Henryk: — And now they steal loudly and on top of that they say it's an investment!

Ola: — I don't want to hear about communism. I want to know if we'll have somewhere to live in half a year. We have a child.

Bartek: — Maybe we should just take the money and move out? Zieliński pays well. Mrs Wanda got…

Mrs Halina: — Mrs Wanda got half of what that flat is worth. And now she lives at her daughter's in Nowa Huta, on the sofa.

Mr Henryk: — And do you know why that is? Because politicians are all one gang. Before the elections they promise, during the campaign they smile, and then the president goes his way, the minister his way and the nation its way. Whoever has the cash has the government on his side.

Mrs Grażyna: — Henryk, you've had the same opinion for forty years and for forty years you've voted for the same party.

Mr Henryk: — Because it's the only party that hasn't lied to me. Yet.

Ola: — Ladies and gentlemen, politics is one thing, and our situation is another. What are we going to do? What options do we have? I don't trust anyone who comes with a business card and a smile. But I don't know if we can win. It's a risk. If we lose, we'll bear the consequences, not him.

Bartek: — He has the advantage. He has money, lawyers, the media. And we have Mrs Halina's cheesecake.

Mrs Halina: — Cheesecake is a power too, young man.

Everyone laughed and the atmosphere got a bit better.

— They want us to give up on our own — she added. — They can't take our flats away from us, so they want to make our lives difficult and limit our access to the cellar: noise, inspections, letters. But I won't back down. And I won't deny myself a good night's sleep because of some Mr Zieliński.

Bartek told us that there was a problem in his company too: the boss wants to sack half the people because the company has a new partner from abroad.

— Money controls everything now — he said. — People don't count.

— There's nothing human about it — Mrs Halina agreed. — But that doesn't mean you have to give up. Nothing is impossible. And we won't beg anyone for mercy.

— What will happen will happen — said Mr Zbyszek. — But we'll be together.

Mr Henryk suggested writing to the mayor. Mrs Grażyna reminded him that Kraków doesn't have a “burmistrz”, but a city president, and that the president has more important things to do, for example his campaign, because in two years he'll be a candidate again.

— Every politician supports the people before the elections — said Mr Henryk. — After the elections he only supports himself.

— But the local press can help us — said Kasia. — If the public finds out, no official will accept the company's plans so easily. Public opinion is a power.

We decided to discuss everything again in a week. Mrs Halina was to represent the tenants in talks with the company.

Mr Zbyszek: — I'm not moving anywhere. I'll die here. At most Zieliński will come to the funeral.

Mr Henryk (to me): — And you, Mr German, what do you think? You have money, you can buy whatever you want.

Everyone looked at me. My heart started beating faster. Speaking Polish to one person is one thing. To seven people who are arguing it's something completely different.

— I… — I began. — I'm not from here. That's true. I've only lived here for a month. But my grandma was born in this house. Her parents lived here, her grandfather owned this house before the war. And the man who sold the rights to Zieliński said he was the only heir. I think he lied. I think we can prove it.

Silence. Even Mr Henryk said nothing.

— How? — asked Ola.

I looked at Mrs Halina. Mrs Halina was looking into her cup.

— I don't know yet — I said. — But we need a good lawyer.

— I have a lawyer — said Mrs Halina suddenly, without raising her eyes. — Mr Lewandowski. He was my husband's friend. He's eighty-two and the most stubborn man in Kraków. He has won cases nobody else wanted to take on.

— How much does he charge? — asked Mr Henryk.

— He won't take anything from me. He has owed me a favour for forty years.

---

At the end we voted. Mrs Halina proposed three things:

1. We don't let anyone from the company into the flats or the cellar without a court decision.
2. We go together to Mr Lewandowski.
3. Kasia will write to a journalist she knows at the “Gazeta Krakowska”, so that everyone in the city knows what's going on.

Everyone was in favour. Mr Henryk too, although he said it wouldn't help anyway. Mrs Grażyna said that if Henryk says it won't help, then it definitely will.

Nobody was against. Mr Henryk said that this made Mrs Halina our leader. Mrs Halina said she didn't want to be any kind of leader, she just wanted some peace. But I think she liked it.

Kasia promised that tomorrow she would send a registered letter to the company and talk to the journalist about working together. Mr Henryk said he could see a connection between Zieliński and half the officials in the city and that everything was connected. Mrs Grażyna said that Henryk sees connections everywhere, even between the weather and the government.

When everyone was leaving, Mrs Halina stopped me in the hall.

— Today you talked about the Nowak family as if you knew something.

— I know less than I'd like to.

— Yes. — She looked at me for a long time. — You have her eyes, you know? Zosia's. When you get angry, you look exactly like her.

And she closed the door before I could answer.
