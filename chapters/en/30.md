# Chapter 30. The Key

_Tuesday, 12 October 2027_

Exactly a year ago I came to Kraków. In the rain, with one suitcase, a letter, a key and Polish that was enough to buy bread. Actually no, it wasn't even enough for that. I remember how in the bakery Mrs Basia asked: “Wheat, rye, wholemeal?”, and I pointed with my finger.

This morning I went to the bakery and said: “Good morning, Mrs Basia, I'd like a loaf of sourdough rye and four kaiser rolls, only not the over-baked ones, because Auntie will complain again.” Mrs Basia said I talk like a born Cracovian. Then she added that Cracovians say “bułki”, not “kajzerki”. I'm still learning.

Between two thousand and twenty-six and two thousand and twenty-seven my whole life changed. A year ago I couldn't even have read this sentence.

I'm writing in the café downstairs, newly reopened after the renovation. Locals and tourists sit around here. Mrs Basia says the coffee here tastes lousy, but people obviously like lousy coffee. The waitress no longer pays any attention to my notebooks. I have an awful lot of them. Given everything that has happened this year, I remain an optimist. It wouldn't be right to complain.

Kasia says I mustn't interfere in her lessons when her students come. For them I'm like a living textbook: a German who has gone the whole way through every possible mistake. Auntie says we can't be separated any more. Bonifacy has taken over the whole tenement. And the renovation is finally finished.

Kasia told me to write a summary at the end of the diary. “Like in an essay,” she said. “Introduction, main part, conclusion. The introduction was a year ago. The main part too. Now the conclusion.” And then she added: “Just don't write too nicely, or nobody will believe it's you.”

I'll try.

Autumn has come. Outside the window the lime tree is turning yellow, and I'm sitting in the café downstairs, by the window I walked past in the rain with a suitcase a year ago. Kasia says I finally have to finish this diary, otherwise I'll be writing it for ever.

---

**What happened to the people.**

Ryszard Zieliński is waiting for his criminal trial in custody. The prosecutor's office has brought seven charges against him. The decision in our case is now final. His company has declared bankruptcy. The tenements he bought in Kazimierz are now being checked by a special commission. In three of them it has already turned out that the documents were false. There's a new article in the “Gazeta Krakowska” every week. The journalist who was in court won a prize for them.

Her articles caused a scandal in the city. Tomek and Ewelina's wedding was the second event of the year. The first event was the verdict in our case.

Patryk Lis confessed to everything and is cooperating with the prosecutor's office. The court sentenced him to four years in prison. Mr Zbyszek was at the hearing. After the verdict he went up to him and said something nobody heard. Patryk cried. Mr Zbyszek has never said what he told him. When I ask, he only says: “Everyone deserves a second chance. But only one.”

Tomek and Ewelina got married in September. The wedding party lasted two days, as is proper in Poland. The parents welcomed them with bread and salt. The guests sang “Sto lat” and shouted “Gorzko!” every five minutes, and then the young couple had to kiss. At midnight there were the “oczepiny”: the bride took off her veil and threw it behind her. Magda caught it. The groom threw his tie. Rui caught it. Now Magda and Rui are going out and pretending it's a coincidence. I danced all night. Kasia says I almost can now. Grandma Tereska danced the polonaise with me and said that “this German does have legs after all”.

Before the wedding Tomek changed three times: first into a dark jacket, then into a grey suit, then back into the jacket. He stood in front of the mirror in Mrs Danuta's living room and asked everyone if he looked serious. I was his best man, so I had to stay with him the whole day and make sure he didn't forget the rings. In church they made their marriage vows. The first kiss as husband and wife was so long that the priest had to clear his throat. Then there was the first dance. Now Tomek is married and says the hardest thing about marriage is sharing food.

The invitation said: “Ewelina and Tomasz have the honour of inviting you to the ceremony of their marriage.” In Polish it's “zawarcie związku małżeńskiego”, concluding a marriage union. Mr Zbyszek said that sounds like a contract with a bank. Tomek said it is a bit like that, only without the option of giving notice. Mrs Danuta added that nobody in her family had ever got divorced and that Tomek wouldn't be the first.

The invitation came by post in June. Ewelina said that marrying a policeman is an adventure for life, and Tomek that marrying a firefighter is a guarantee that there will never be a fire at home.

At the wedding Tomek proposed a toast: “To friendship, which is stronger than any fire. To loyalty. And to promises that are kept.” Everyone congratulated the young couple. In the morning, when the guests were saying goodbye, Mrs Danuta saw each of them off with a jar of bigos, just as she had welcomed each of them with bread.

Mr Henryk and Mrs Grażyna are still arguing. But in June they celebrated their fiftieth wedding anniversary, their golden wedding, and at the party Mr Henryk made a speech in which he said that in fifty years he had not won a single argument with his wife and that these had been the happiest years of his life. Mrs Grażyna said that wasn't true, because he had won once, in eighty-seven. Nobody knows what it was about.

Ola and Bartek stayed on the fourth floor. Little Zosia is starting to walk. The first word she said was “kot”, cat. Bonifacy was very proud.

Ola is pregnant again and the baby is due in December. She says this time it will be a boy and they'll call him Józef, after Auntie's husband. Tiny Zosia doesn't know yet that she'll have a brother.

Mrs Wanda came back from Nowa Huta to her old flat on the first floor. On the first day she baked a gateau for all the tenants and cried the whole evening.

Mr Lewandowski has retired. He says the case of seven Lipowa Street was his last and his best. Every Thursday he comes to Auntie's for tea and they play cards. The lawyer loses. He claims he does it on purpose.

Mrs Danuta has put a new table in the “Pod Aniołem” bar, by the window, with a small sign: “The Nowak family's table”. Nobody may sit there without a reservation. Only Auntie has a reservation.

In June Mrs Danuta won the city competition for the best pierogi in Kraków. She says it's not a success, it's justice. Since that day, Auntie says, every customer there is treated like a member of the family. Grandma Tereska says Kraków is paradise on earth, you just have to know how to find it.

---

**What happened to me.**

I live in Kraków. I have a Polish PESEL number, a Polish company, a Polish bank account, a Polish accountant, a Polish cat (officially he's Auntie's cat, but he sleeps at my place) and a Polish fiancée. We'll get married in May, in the church in Kazimierz, and the reception will be at the “Pod Aniołem” bar. Mrs Danuta is already planning the menu. It's six pages long.

The company is doing well. Michael has raised my rate, and since September I've had an assistant, a computer science student from AGH, who is better than me at everything, even at Polish, because he's from Ukraine. My first pay in Poland was small, but it's my profession and I like it. I just have to work on not working at weekends. Meanwhile Kasia has completed a course for examiners.

In June I passed the state exam in Polish at level B2. In the oral part the examiner asked me why I'm learning Polish. I told him the whole story in fifteen minutes. At the end the examiner said it wasn't an answer to the question but a novel, and that he was giving me the maximum number of points.

My mum is coming to the wedding with Jim. Jim is learning Polish with an app. He can already say: “Two beers, please” and “Where is the toilet?” He says that's enough for a wedding. He's right.

Kasia and I agree on almost everything. Except commas.

---

**What happened to Auntie.**

Auntie Halina turned eighty-one in August. At first I got the date wrong and turned up with a cake a week too early. At the birthday party there were forty people, three cakes and one cat. Auntie said it was her best birthday since nineteen seventy.

Almost every day she goes for a walk by the Vistula. Once a week she takes the tram to Rakowicki Cemetery, to her parents' grave. Zosia is buried in Hamburg, next to Grandpa Klaus, but Auntie had her name added to the stone in Kraków. Now the Nowak grave says:

_“Stanisław Nowak, 1912–1978. Maria Nowak, née Lis, 1918–1990. Zofia Weber, née Nowak, 1942–2026. Together forever.”_

Auntie left space for one more name. She said she's in no hurry, but she likes to have everything ready.

In the cellar, in the place where the cavity was, there is now a small brass plaque. Auntie thought for a long time about what to write on it. In the end she wrote what her dad said:

_“The truth can only be hidden. And wait. — S.N., 1971”._

And underneath, in smaller letters:

_“It waited 55 years. Two sisters and one cat found it.”_

I protested that it isn't true, because one sister was dead, and I was the one who found it. Auntie said that this is exactly the truth, only a kind that can't be proved in court.

---

**What happened to the keys.**

Both keys hang in Auntie's kitchen, on one nail, above the table. The red ribbon and the green one. Sometimes, when there's a draught, they knock against each other and make a quiet, metallic sound. Auntie says that's Zosia and her talking.

Kasia still wears her small silver key. She says she'll only take it off on our wedding day. Then she'll hang it on the Bernatka footbridge, together with a padlock with our names on it. But she won't throw the key into the Vistula.

— Because you never know — she says — whether you'll need to open something.

---

**What happened to the language.**

A year ago I knew maybe a thousand words. Kasia says I now know several thousand. I haven't counted. But I know that I learnt the most important things not from a textbook, but from people. “Be careful” from Mr Zbyszek. “Deal” from Kasia. “Eat something” from Mrs Danuta. “Together forever” from Grandma's mother, whom I never met. “Don't lock anything up in a wall” from Grandma.

And four words from the note in the dictionary.

A year ago I thought I was learning Polish to understand Grandma. Now I know that's not everything. I was learning Polish so that people could tell me something. So that Auntie could tell me her story in her own language. So that Kasia could tell me she wasn't pretending. So that Mr Władysław in the hospital in Zakopane could tell me about his father, who led people across the mountains. So that Edward Lis could tell me the truth before he died.

Language is a key. Every word opens some door. Some open easily. Others are rusty and you have to turn them together, the two of you, with all your strength. But behind every door there is a person.

That's the most important lesson of my life: home isn't a place you're given. It's a place you make.

Grandma wrote: “The key opens what I locked.”

Now I know what she locked. Not a box in a wall. Herself. And now I also know what opened it.

I love. I miss. I'm sorry. I forgive.

The end.
