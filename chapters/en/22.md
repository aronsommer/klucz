# Chapter 22. The Fire

_Monday, 1 March 2027, morning_

I'm writing in Mrs Danuta's kitchen. It's seven in the morning. I haven't slept all night. My hands smell of smoke and I think they'll go on smelling of it for another week. Everyone is alive. That's the most important thing. I'll write it once more, so that I believe it: everyone is alive.

---

Bonifacy woke me up. It was twenty past three. He was scratching my face with his paw and miaowing like never before: loud, high, desperate. I wanted to push him away and go on sleeping. Then I smelled it.

Smoke.

I jumped out of bed. In the flat it was still clear, but grey smoke was coming in under the front door. I touched the handle. It was warm. Not hot, but warm. I opened the door a few centimetres. The staircase was full of smoke. From below came a strange sound, as if someone were crumpling paper. And light. Orange light on the walls.

The fire was downstairs. By the entrance to the cellar.

I closed the door. I called one one two. I spoke fast, but clearly: fire, seven Lipowa Street, a tenement, four tenants upstairs, an elderly woman, a small child, one man in the cellar. The dispatcher repeated the address and said the fire brigade was already on its way. That I shouldn't go down the stairs through the smoke, but close the door, block the gaps with wet towels and wait by the window.

But Mr Zbyszek was in the cellar.

The smoke detector in the hall didn't go off at all. Later it turned out that someone had taken the battery out. There's no alarm in the tenement, because the tenement is a hundred and twenty years old. There's no emergency exit either. There's only one staircase and an old ladder in the courtyard.

---

I don't remember everything. I remember wetting a towel and wrapping it round my face. That I knocked on Auntie's door, and she opened at once, wearing a coat over her dressing gown, already in her slippers, with a bag of documents in her hand, as if she had been waiting for this for years. That I shouted upstairs to Ola and Bartek not to come down, but to go out onto the balcony. That Mr Henryk shouted from the ground floor that he was already in the courtyard, and Mrs Grażyna shouted that Henryk had gone out in nothing but his briefs.

I remember sirens. First one, far away, then three, four, closer and closer. Blue lights on the walls of the tenement opposite.

The firefighters came into the gateway in masks, with hoses, with air cylinders on their backs. One of them, small and fast, ran straight towards the cellar. When she passed under the lamp, I saw a plait sticking out from under the helmet.

It was Ewelina.

The firefighters switched off the electricity that powered the whole tenement and cut off the gas. One of them shouted: “Hurry up! Quick!” Another shielded us with his body as we went down the stairs. Behind us something was burning and crackling. Ewelina told me later that their first job is to protect people, and only then the building.

Other firefighters led us down the stairs. One of them led Auntie and me through the smoke, with masks on our faces. “Don't let go of my hand!” he kept repeating. Ola, Bartek and little Zosia (yes, they called their daughter Zosia, I found out that night) were taken down from the balcony by ladder. The little one didn't cry at all. She was asleep. Bartek cried for her.

Earlier Ola had called for help from the balcony so loudly that the whole street heard her.

We stood in the street in blankets the firefighters gave us. It was minus eight. Auntie was holding Bonifacy under her blanket. Bonifacy was holding on to Auntie with all four paws.

All around was chaos: sirens, shouting, neighbours from other tenements in their pyjamas, someone filming everything on their phone. The sight of the flames in the cellar was terrifying. Later the fire chief said a disaster had only been avoided because I called so quickly.

— Where's Mr Zbyszek? — Auntie kept repeating. — Where's Mr Zbyszek?

After ten minutes, the longest of my life, Ewelina came out of the gateway. She was dragging a big man behind her. Mr Zbyszek was black with soot, coughing and swearing. Swearing so much that even the firefighters blushed. It was the most beautiful sound I had ever heard.

A paramedic cleaned the wound on his arm. The injuries weren't dangerous, but painful.

— You could have died — said the paramedic.

— Good God! — said Mr Zbyszek. — The first time in my life someone has rescued me, and not me someone else.

The paramedics put him on a stretcher, gave him oxygen and took him to the ambulance. He has burns on his hands and smoke poisoning, but he'll live. When they were putting him in the ambulance, he took off his mask and shouted to me:

— Mr Jonas! The wall! The wall has cracked!

Ewelina risked her own life to pull him out.

Mr Zbyszek also had a bump on his head, because in the dark he had hit it on a low beam. But he escaped with his life. Everyone did.

The fire spared the flats, but the damage in the cellar was enormous. An expert said later that the effect was like that of a small explosive charge. The firefighters who kept arriving in more engines couldn't believe that nobody had been killed. The rescue came at the last moment.

---

They were putting out the fire for two hours. The fire started by the cellar door and went downwards, along the old wooden stairs, the storeroom doors, the crates and bikes. It didn't get upstairs, to the flats. The firefighters say it's a miracle. And that it's thanks to the tenement being old, with thick walls. A modern building would have burned like a match.

At one point something exploded in the cellar. A loud bang, flames in the small windows by the pavement. The firefighters shouted to each other over the radio. Later they said it was a gas cylinder in someone's storeroom. If the explosion had been bigger, half the building could have collapsed.

When it was all over, at six in the morning, Tomek arrived. A man in plain clothes also came, with a torch and a case. A fire investigator. He went down to the cellar, was there for twenty minutes, came out and said one word:

— Arson.

By the cellar door they found a plastic petrol can. Someone had poured petrol on the stairs and set it alight. Someone knew that Mr Zbyszek was sleeping in the cellar. Someone knew that the notary was coming in the morning.

The investigator also established what caused the fire: petrol and an ordinary lighter. The fire didn't reach us only because the old oak cellar door burned very slowly.

Tomek had a face I had never seen on him before. Cold.

— This isn't a break-in any more — he said. — This is attempted murder. Now Zieliński has a problem.

---

Then we went down to the cellar. Me, Tomek and Ewelina, in her helmet and with a torch. The firefighters only allowed us five minutes, because the structure might be unstable.

The cellar was black. Everything was black: the walls, the ceiling, the floor, what was left of the doors. It smelled of burning, wet ash and something chemical. Water stood ankle-deep on the floor. Auntie Halina's jars had cracked from the heat. Gherkins lay in the ash.

When my eyes got used to the darkness, I saw that the passage to the old part of the cellar was almost blocked with rubble.

At the end of the corridor, behind the brick arch, the torchlight hit the wall.

The old wall, the grey one, had survived. But the younger wall, the one of darker bricks, hadn't withstood the heat and the cold water. It had cracked. A wide crack ran through the middle. A few bricks had fallen onto the floor. Behind them was darkness. A small space in the wall, like a chamber or a cupboard.

Ewelina pointed the torch into it.

Inside, on a stone shelf, stood a box. Metal, black with soot, about the size of a big book. It had reinforced corners and a handle on top. And at the front it had a lock.

A keyhole.

None of us said anything. Tomek took out his phone and took twenty photos. Then he said nobody was to touch it until the notary came, and he posted a policeman at the entrance to the cellar.

---

Now it's half past seven. Kasia is asleep on the sofa next to me. She came at four, in her pyjamas and a jacket, and since then she hasn't said a word to me, she's just held my hand. Auntie Halina is asleep in Mrs Danuta's bedroom. Mrs Danuta is frying pancakes for everyone, because, as she says, “after a fire you have to eat something sweet”.

At twelve Mr Kaczmarek, the notary, will come. The lawyer already knows. So does the photographer from the newspaper. The lawyer said on the phone:

— Zieliński wanted to burn the evidence. And he opened the door for us. History likes jokes like that, Mr Weber.

I have the key in my pocket. I didn't take it out once all night. But I know it's there. I can feel its weight.
