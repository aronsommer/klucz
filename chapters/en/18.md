# Chapter 18. Zakopane

_Saturday, 16 January 2027, in the evening_

Auntie Halina read the letter on the first of January, at three in the morning, alone, with a glass of champagne. In the morning she came to me with eyes red from crying and only said:

— She forgave me. Already in seventy-two. And for fifty years I was afraid to open the envelope.

She didn't show me the letter. She said it was her letter and maybe one day. But she told me one thing that comes out of it: behind the wall in the cellar there is something that “belongs to both of us and to those who come after us”. Grandma wrote that Dad, that is her and Auntie's father, hid there “everything they couldn't take from us”.

— We're opening that wall — said Auntie. — But properly. Not like thieves in the night.

Mr Lewandowski, the lawyer, was of the same opinion, only he expressed it at greater length. If there's a document in the wall and we just take it out, Zieliński's lawyers will say in court that we forged it. That's why the wall has to be opened officially: in the presence of a notary who will describe everything in a report, with a photographer, with witnesses. Mr Kaczmarek, the notary, agreed to come on Monday, the first of February, at ten in the morning.

Until then Mr Zbyszek is guarding the wall. He sleeps in the cellar on a mattress, with a thermos and a hammer. He says he slept like that in the army and for him it's like a holiday.

And I went to the mountains with Kasia and Tomek. Kasia said that if I didn't have a rest, I'd go mad. She was right. For a week I've been dreaming of bricks.

---

From Kraków you go to Zakopane by bus or by train. The train is prettier, but slower. The bus is faster, but the driver drives like a madman. We went by bus.

The distance from Kraków to Zakopane is only a hundred kilometres, but on a Friday morning the trip takes three hours. The driver drove at a speed that frightened even Tomek, and Tomek is a policeman. At every stop someone got off, and once the driver went through a red light.

— Relax — said Tomek. — I'm off duty.

After two hours, instead of fields and small towns, mountains appeared outside the window. Real ones, high, white with snow, with sharp peaks against a very blue sky.

— The Tatras — said Kasia proudly, as if she had made them herself.

Zakopane is a small town, but in winter crowds come here. On the main street, Krupówki, there were lots of tourists, souvenir shops, restaurants and food stalls. It smelled of smoke, mulled wine and cheese. The cheese is oscypek: hard, smoked, spindle-shaped, made from sheep's milk. You eat it warm, with cranberries. I ate three.

We stayed in a wooden house belonging to Mrs Józefa, an old highland woman. The house had a steep roof, carved windows and a fireplace in every room. Mrs Józefa spoke “gwara”, the dialect of the highlanders. At first I understood almost nothing.

— Hey, you've come from far away, haven't you! Sit down, just don't stand in the doorway, the cold's coming in. Will you have some kwaśnica?

Kasia translated for me into “normal Polish”: “dyć” means “przecież”, after all, “ino” means “tylko”, only, and kwaśnica is a soup made from sauerkraut. A “góral” is a man from the mountains, a “góralka” is a woman. A “baca” is a highlander who grazes sheep and makes cheese.

— But have you brought a Kraut here? — asked Mrs Józefa, looking at me.

— Mrs Józefa, he's from Hamburg — said Tomek. — But he's a decent lad.

— Well, I can see he's decent. Only thin. — She put a huge plate of kwaśnica in front of me. — Eat, son. In the mountains you have to have strength.

Every Polish woman over sixty tells me I'm thin. I'm starting to believe it.

---

On Friday we took the funicular up Gubałówka. From there you can see the whole Tatras, from one end to the other. Kasia pointed out the peaks to me: Giewont, which looks like a sleeping knight, Kasprowy Wierch, Świnica, Rysy, the highest peak in Poland. On the other side of the mountains is Slovakia.

Trails run through the mountains: red, blue, yellow, green. Each colour is a different route. Kasia says you mustn't leave the path, because wild animals live in the Tatras: chamois, marmots, wolves and even bears. Every year a dozen or so tourists die in the mountains: from avalanches, storms, falling rocks and plain stupidity. There are also lots of caves under Giewont. In one of them, the legend says, knights are sleeping who will wake up when Poland is in danger.

An eagle was circling above the peaks, with its wings spread wide. Kasia says several kinds of eagle live in the Tatras and that it's one of the few places in Poland where nature is still really wild. Kasia says that in summer flocks of sheep with bells graze in the mountain pastures, and big white dogs wag their tails and protect them from wolves. The highlanders look after the environment, because they live from it.

— One day we'll climb Rysy — said Kasia. — In summer. When you're fit.

— When will I be fit?

— Never, if you eat so many oscypki.

---

This morning we went skiing. Tomek skis brilliantly, Kasia well, I had skied twice in my life, in Austria, ten years ago. We hired the equipment: skis, boots, poles, a helmet, goggles. The man at the hire shop looked at me and said: “If I were you, I'd start on the nursery slope.” In Polish it's called “ośla łączka”, the donkey meadow: the slope for children and beginners. My honour didn't allow it.

We set off at dawn. I wanted to sleep a bit longer, but Tomek said the best snow is in the morning. It was a beautiful, frosty morning. There was no signal on the slope, so everyone switched off their phones.

We took the lift to the top. The slope was wide, white and from the top it looked very steep. Tomek went down first, fast and elegant. Kasia after him. I stood at the top and thought about the fact that I don't have a will.

Earlier, on a flat bit by the lift, Tomek had told me that skiing is like riding a bike: once you learn, you never forget. Tomek was lying. I made my first attempt at a turn and landed in the snow straight away.

— You can do it! — Kasia shouted from below. — Just don't hurry!

At the bottom of the slope there was a race for children. It went ahead despite the frost and the wind. Little skiers in helmets started one after another, and the parents shouted: “Faster! Go!” The winner of the race was maybe six and was crying because he wanted chocolate, not a medal. Tomek said that once he had wanted to become a professional skier, but he preferred playing at policemen.

In the first round the judge shouted “Start!” to each child, and the child went over a good part of the course on the flat.

Kasia said that on this slope there was no way back: either you go down, or you go down. Tomek appeared next to me and said I would be a champion. I believed in my abilities. That was a mistake.

The first run was good. Slow, careful, but good. The second was even better. The third was too good. I felt confident. I went faster. Too fast. Halfway down the slope I saw a little boy in front of me who suddenly fell over. I turned to avoid him. My skis went one way, I went the other.

After the fall I only remember white snow, blue sky, white snow again. Then a terrible pain in my leg. Then nothing.

---

When I opened my eyes, a rescuer in a red jacket with “TOPR” on it was kneeling over me. That's the mountain rescue service. Next to him stood Kasia, as white as snow.

— Please don't move — said the rescuer calmly. — What's your name?

— Jonas.

— Do you know where you are?

— In Zakopane. On the slope. The nursery slope would have been better.

The rescuer smiled.

— There's a sense of humour. That's a good sign. Where does it hurt?

— My leg. The right one. A lot.

The rescuer gently touched my leg through my trousers. I screamed.

— Probably a fracture. We're taking you down and to hospital. The boy is fine, nothing happened to him. If you hadn't avoided him, it would have been worse. For him.

When the rescuers pulled my boot off, my leg was swollen and blue.

They took me down on a special sledge. Then by ambulance to the hospital in Zakopane. Kasia rode with me and held my hand the whole time. Tomek followed us in Mrs Józefa's car, with my skis and my honour.

The rescuer said that if it had been high up in the mountains and not on the slope, a helicopter would have come for me. I asked if I could still change the place of the accident. He didn't laugh.

The TOPR rescuers bring injured people down from the mountains almost every day. They brought me down too, though only from the slope. The rescuer said that once every young highlander wanted to join them, and now they're short of people. They work at night, in storms, with no shelter from the wind. Nobody guarantees them that they'll come back.

Now it's evening. I'm lying in hospital with my leg in plaster. A broken shinbone. The doctor says an operation won't be necessary, but that for six weeks I'll walk on crutches.

Six weeks. And on the first of February we're supposed to open the wall.

Kasia is sitting next to me and sleeping on a chair with her head on my bed. When we arrived, she cried and shouted at me at the same time. I didn't know you could do that. Then she said that if I ever do something so stupid again, she'll break my other leg herself.

I think that means she loves me.
