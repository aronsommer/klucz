# Chapter 1. The Key

_Monday, 12 October 2026_

My name is Jonas. I'm German. I'm twenty-eight years old and I'm a programmer. I have been living in Kraków for a few days.

Today is Monday, the twelfth of October, two thousand and twenty-six. That's how you say it in Polish. Long, isn't it? The weather is lousy, but I'm happy. I like Kraków very much.

This is my first day in Poland. My Polish is still weak. I know a few words, but I speak very slowly and I often don't understand what people say to me. That's why I'm writing this diary. In Polish. My grandma always used to say: “If you want to understand something, you have to write it down.”

I can't write well yet, because I know quite few words and only a few forms. But never mind. Here is my diary.

My grandma was Polish. Her name was Zofia, but everyone called her Zosia. She is no longer alive. She died in August, in a hospital in Hamburg. Now I'm alone. I have no brother or sister. My father died a long time ago, and my mother lives in America and rarely calls.

Unfortunately, I've been a bit lonely lately. Maybe that will change in Kraków.

Grandma left me her flat, here in Kraków, on Lipowa Street, a letter and an old key.

There is only one sentence in the letter:

_“The key opens what I locked.”_

I don't know what it means. Nobody knows.

---

In the morning I take the train from Berlin. I don't fly, because I don't like airports. A train ticket is also cheaper than a plane ticket, and a flight with a change takes almost as long. The journey takes a lot of time. Next to me sits a little girl with her mum. She watches me the whole time, and in the end she asks:

— Are you from Germany?

— Yes — I say. — How do you know?

— Because you talk so funny.

Her mum says: “Sorry!”, but I laugh. The girl is right.

In Kraków it's cold and raining. I take a taxi. The driver asks: “Where are we going?”

— Seven Lipowa Street — I say.

— Kazimierz! A beautiful place. Do you work here?

— No. I mean, yes. I work from home, on the computer.

— I see. And why did you come to Poland?

— My grandma was from here.

The driver nods. I think he doesn't understand everything, but he's nice.

The car drives through the centre. I see a park, an old church, lots of people with umbrellas and loads of birds in the square.

---

The house on Lipowa Street is old and yellow. It's a tenement house. It has four floors, big windows and a heavy door. Downstairs there is a small shop and a café.

Grandma's flat is on the second floor. I open the door with the new key I got from the notary. Inside it's dark and quiet. There is dust everywhere. Slowly I look at the old furniture, the many books and the photos on the wall. In the room there is a big bed, and in the kitchen a table and two chairs. From the window I can see the courtyard and one tree.

That is now my favourite view. Right under the window, in the tree, a black bird is sitting and looking at me.

In the photos there is a young woman. It's Grandma. She is beautiful and smiling. Next to her there is another girl, a bit younger. Who is she? I don't know.

I take the old key out of my pocket. It's heavy and has a red ribbon. I try to open the door of the flat with it, the bathroom and the kitchen, and then the wardrobe. Nothing. It doesn't fit anywhere.

Someone knocks. I open. In the corridor there is an elderly lady. She has grey hair and glasses, and she is holding a pot in her hands. Next to her sits a huge ginger cat.

— Hello — she says. — Are you Zosia's grandson?

— Yes. Hello. I'm Jonas.

The lady looks at me for a long time. I don't know why. Then she says:

— Halina Kowalska. I live next door. This is Bonifacy. — She points at the cat. — And this is soup. Tomato soup. I'm sure you haven't eaten anything.

— Thank you very much! Did you know my grandma?

Mrs Halina says nothing. She gives me the pot and goes back to her flat. Bonifacy stays. He sits down on my bed as if it were his home.

— Bonifacy, come here, darling! — Mrs Halina calls from the corridor. But the cat doesn't go. — Oh well — says Mrs Halina. — He always does what he wants.

The soup is very good.

Now it's night. I'm writing at the kitchen table. I'm tired, but somehow happy. At last I'm here.

Tomorrow I have to buy bread, milk and coffee. And I have to find a Polish language school. I mustn't forget that.

The old key is lying on the table. I mustn't lose it. Grandma didn't write why it is so important. I have no idea what to do with it. But I'll find out soon.

But above all I want to know: what does this key open?
