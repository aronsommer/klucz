# Chapter 3. The First Lesson

_Thursday, 15 October 2026_

In the morning I knock on Mrs Halina's door. I want to give back the pot. And I want to ask about the key.

Mrs Halina opens the door only a little.

— Good morning, Mrs Halina. Thank you for the soup. It was very good.

— You're welcome. — She takes the pot.

— I have one more question. Mr Zbyszek says that you know this key. — I show her the key.

Mrs Halina looks at the key. She is as white as a sheet.

— Mr Zbyszek talks a lot — she answers. — I don't know anything about any key. Goodbye.

The door closes. Bonifacy looks at me as if he wanted to say: “Not like that, stupid.”

Maybe he's right. Maybe I ask too fast. Maybe here you have to wait. Grandma always used to say: “Wait and you'll see.”

---

At five I'm on Józefa Street. I ring. A young woman with a mug of tea in her hand opens the door. She has short dark hair and big green eyes.

— Mr Jonas? Come in. I'm Kasia.

Her flat is small, but very bright. There are books, flowers and little notes with Polish words everywhere. On the bathroom door there is a note: “łazienka”. On the fridge: “lodówka”. On the cat sleeping on the sofa there is no note.

— Coffee or tea? — asks Kasia.

— Tea, please.

We sit down at the table. Kasia opens a notebook.

— Go ahead, ask whatever you want. Any question is a good one. I'll be back in a moment, I'm just putting the water on.

She comes back before I can think of anything.

— Right. First some simple questions. What's your name?

— My name is Jonas Weber.

— Where are you from?

— I'm from Hamburg. It's in Germany, in the north.

— I know where Hamburg is — Kasia laughs. — What do you do?

— I'm a programmer. I write programs for companies. I work from home.

— Why are you learning Polish?

That's a difficult question. I think for a moment.

— My grandma was Polish. When I was small, she spoke to me in Polish. I answered in German. Now she's dead, and I… I want to understand her. I know, it's stupid.

Kasia stops writing. She looks at me.

— It's not stupid. It's the best reason I've heard this year. — She closes the notebook. — Listen, can we say “ty” to each other? I'm twenty-seven, people only call me “pani” at the council office.

— “Ty”? All right. I'm Jonas.

— I know. And I'm Kasia. Now pay attention, because this is important: in Poland we say “pan” and “pani” to people we don't know, to older people, in a shop, at the doctor's, at the post office. To family, to friends and to children we say “ty”. If you don't know, say “pan” or “pani”. Better too polite than not polite enough.

— I understand.

— And now tell me what you did yesterday.

— Yesterday… — I don't know how. — Yesterday I do…

— “Robiłem” — says Kasia. — The past tense. Ja robiłem, ty robiłeś, on robił. And me, because I'm a woman: ja robiłam.

— Yesterday I went shopping. I bought bread. And a fridge.

— A fridge? — Kasia laughs. — You're fast.

Then Kasia asks if I want an intensive course or a relaxed one. She explains the difference to me: intensive means four lessons a week, tests and lots of exercises. Relaxed means one lesson and lots of conversation.

— Honestly? — I say. — I don't know if I can study intensively. I work all day.

— Then relaxed. But with one rule: in the lesson we only talk in Polish. Even if it's hard.

— And if I don't know a word?

— Then you show it with your hands. Or you draw it. My best student only drew for the whole first month.

— You're joking.

— I'm joking a little. — Kasia smiles. — I studied Polish philology, then I taught at a secondary school, and now I teach adults. Adults are worse than kids. Kids aren't embarrassed.

I ask what Polish philology is. Kasia says it's the study of the Polish language and literature. And that it's funny how little use it is in real life.

When I thank her, she says: “The pleasure is all mine.” I carefully write the sentence down in my notebook. I'll be glad to use it.

— What does “lecz” mean? — I ask. I saw the word in a newspaper.

— The same as “ale”. Only “lecz” comes from books. Nobody talks like that, unless they're a poet or a president.

— And “zatem”?

— The same as “więc”. Also from books. Polish is sort of two languages: one for talking, the other for writing.

— My brain can't take this.

— It can. The brain is a muscle. — Kasia smiles. — And don't worry so much. You're doing quite well. In half a year you'll speak wonderfully.

— I'm not sure.

— I am. Do you realise how much you already know?

It's a strange feeling: a stranger believes in me more than I do myself.

— What's your method? — I ask.

— My theory is simple. A language isn't a set of rules, it's a skill. Like riding a bike. Rules are important, sure, but first you have to speak, and only then understand why. Grammar is an explanation, not a recipe.

— And if I speak badly?

— But of course you'll speak badly. It's a natural part of learning. Every word, every mistake is experience. I have only two conditions: a little courage and a lot more tea. Understanding comes with time. The mind likes repetition, and thinking in a foreign language begins after a few months.

— And one more thing — she adds. — My technique: turn on Polish radio every day, even if you don't understand anything.

— One of my friends in Hamburg would say it's an unusual method.

— I suppose so. I suggest you don't listen to your friends in Hamburg. Our classes are on Thursdays.

— What interested you most in Kraków? — Kasia asks too.

— Everything. It's a very interesting city.

— “Interesujące” is a textbook word. Say “ciekawe”.

— Absolutely “ciekawe”.

— Excellent. — Kasia laughs. — It doesn't surprise me that you like getting to know new places. I'll be glad to show them to you.

---

Near the end of the lesson someone opens the door without knocking. A tall man in a police uniform comes into the flat. He goes straight to the kitchen.

— Hi, sis! Have you got anything to eat? — He opens the fridge. — You've got nothing. As always.

— That's my brother, Tomek — says Kasia. — He's a policeman. And he's always hungry. Tomek, this is Jonas, my new student from Germany.

Tomek shakes my hand. His hand is as hard as stone.

— Hey. Where do you live?

— At seven Lipowa Street.

— On Lipowa? — Tomek stops eating his apple. — In the tenement house that Zieliński wants to buy?

— Who?

— Ryszard Zieliński. A businessman. He buys old houses in Kazimierz, throws people out and makes hotels. — Tomek makes an angry face. — Watch out for him.

First Mr Zbyszek says: “Watch out for Mrs Halina.” Now Tomek says: “Watch out for Zieliński.” I have the feeling that in Kraków I have to watch out for everyone.

Tomek says he has been a “glina” for eight years. “Glina” also means policeman, but only in everyday speech, like “cop”. Kasia says that in her class every student knows the word from films, but nobody knows that “glina” is also clay, the material pots are made from.

Kasia also says that Tomek plays in the police football team and that he's the goalkeeper because he doesn't like running. Tomek says that's not true. He's the goalkeeper because it's the best position for watching everyone.

Tomek says his sister is a girl with character. Kasia says her brother is a boy with an appetite.

At the end Kasia gives me homework.

— You're writing a diary, right? Good. From tomorrow you write in the past tense. What happened, what you did, what someone said. Every day. Deal?

— Deal.

— And one more thing — says Kasia at the door. — What have you got in your pocket?

— How do you know I've got something?

Kasia smiles.

— Because you kept your hand there the whole lesson. Like a child with a treasure.

I show her the key. Kasia takes it and looks at it under the lamp.

— Beautiful. Very old. — She gives it back to me. — When you find out what it opens, tell me. I like secrets.
