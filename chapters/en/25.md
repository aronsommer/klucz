# Chapter 25. Warsaw

_Friday, 9 April 2027, on the train from Warsaw to Kraków_

The Pendolino goes at two hundred kilometres an hour. Fields, forests, villages, churches and petrol stations flash past the window. Kasia is asleep with her head on my shoulder. Tomek is sitting opposite writing a report on his laptop, with one finger, very slowly, and swearing under his breath. I'm writing my diary. We have two hours and twenty minutes. That should be enough to describe the strangest two days of my life. After the fire, of course.

---

We went on Wednesday morning. Tomek on duty: the Warsaw police had received information that Patryk Lis was hiding at a mate's in Wola, and asked their Kraków colleagues for help, because they're the ones handling the arson case. Kasia and I privately. That is: officially privately. We had the address of Edward Lis, the supposed heir, from the case files. The lawyer said he couldn't forbid us to talk to him. But he also said he couldn't advise it. That was, as I understood it, the lawyer's way of saying “go”.

Warsaw is different from Kraków. Bigger, faster, louder, more modern. Kraków is like an old aunt who tells stories. Warsaw is like her nephew who works for a corporation and has no time. Tall glass skyscrapers, wide streets, traffic jams, the metro, people in suits with coffee in their hands. And right in the middle, between the skyscrapers, the Palace of Culture and Science: huge, heavy, in the Soviet style, with a tower and a clock.

— A present from Stalin — said Kasia. — People in Warsaw say the most beautiful view in Warsaw is from its terrace. Because it's the only place from which you can't see the Palace.

The metro was full of people with laptops. The passengers were looking at their phones. Nobody was talking. Kasia said that in Warsaw people have no time to talk, because everyone is building a career: at a bank, at a corporation, at a ministry, at some foreign company. I said it's the same in Hamburg. Kasia said that's why I'm in Kraków.

But the Old Town surprised me. Colourful houses, narrow streets, the Market Square, the Royal Castle, Sigismund's Column. Everything looks several hundred years old. And everything is new. During the war, after the Warsaw Uprising, the Germans blew up almost the whole city. Street by street, house by house. After the war the people of Warsaw rebuilt the Old Town from the rubble, using old paintings, photos and drawings by architecture students. Stone by stone.

Kasia says that Warsaw has two faces: a modern one, with the glass headquarters of banks, and another one, with the memory of the war. Today the Palace of Culture has cinemas, theatres and scientific institutes. And the insurgents are national heroes.

I stood in the Market Square and thought that it's very Polish: to build everything again, exactly the same, out of stubbornness. To show that we can't be destroyed.

Kasia says that the Old Town is on the UNESCO World Heritage list and is a symbol of reconstruction. The area that was rebuilt is huge. The amount of work the people of Warsaw did is hard even to imagine.

Then we went to the Warsaw Uprising Museum. I don't know how to describe it. Photos, films, weapons, armbands in white and red, letters that children wrote to their parents from the barricades. The sound of bombs in a dark corridor. Sixty-three days of fighting. Two hundred thousand dead. I came out and for ten minutes I couldn't say anything.

In one room there was a real insurgent's rifle, next to it a box of ammunition, and on the wall a list of the units and their commanders. Many of the officers were twenty. Many of the battalion commanders were not much over thirty. I can't imagine what it's like to be twenty and go to the barricade. The insurgents were badly armed: each soldier had only a few bullets. As Kasia said, they fought not with weapons, but with courage.

I thought about Antoni Nowak, who never existed, but who according to the company's documents was killed right there. And I felt sick. Because someone took the real deaths of two hundred thousand people and used them to make money from a lie.

---

Edward Lis lives in Bródno, on the east bank of the Vistula, on a big housing estate from the seventies. Grey blocks, ten storeys each, playgrounds, garages, a small grocery shop with bars on the windows. The staircase smelled of cabbage and cigarettes. The lift didn't work. Of course.

Tomek took us there before he went to the police station. It wasn't far from the metro station.

— It's usually safe here — he said. — But don't come here at night. This isn't an area for tourists.

Flat number forty-seven, seventh floor. Kasia rang the bell. For a long time nobody opened. Then we heard slow steps, a cough and the sound of a chain.

An old man stood in the doorway. Very thin, in a baggy jumper, with an oxygen tube under his nose. Behind him, in the hall, stood an oxygen cylinder on wheels.

— What do you want? — he asked.

— Mr Edward — said Kasia calmly. — We've come from Kraków. From seven Lipowa Street.

Edward Lis looked at her, then at me. For a long time. Then his face changed. I don't know if it was fear or relief.

— You're Zosia's grandson — he said. — You've got her nose.

He let us in.

---

The flat was dark and stuffy. There were medicines, newspapers and old pizza boxes everywhere. The television was on without sound. On the wall hung a calendar from two thousand and twenty-three.

We talked for two hours. Edward Lis spoke slowly, because every so often he ran out of breath. He has lung cancer. The doctors give him a few months.

He told us everything.

Four years ago a man from Zieliński came to him. Polite, elegant, with a briefcase. He knew that Edward had debts. Big debts, at banks, with loan sharks, with friends. He knew he was ill. He offered him a deal. Edward only had to sign a few documents, appear once or twice at a notary's and in court, and he would get two hundred thousand złoty. Others would prepare the documents. A baptism certificate, a death certificate, witnesses. He only had to know what the people in the family were called, where they lived, what they did. And he knew. After all, he had come to Lipowa Street as a child.

Edward Lis said debts had ruined his life. First gambling, then loans, then loans to pay off the loans. His wife left, his children stopped calling. He was left alone, with his illness and a calendar from two thousand and twenty-three.

— They invented Antoni in front of me — he said. — In a law office in Mokotów. One of them said: “Let him be killed in the Uprising, then nobody will check, the archives burned.” The other said: “Let's give him a daughter, Jadwiga, like your mother.” They laughed. And I sat there and only thought about paying off my debts.

— They gave me a cheque — he said. — Two hundred thousand. I knew it was illegal. I knew I wanted to deceive the court and two old women. But I told myself I hadn't robbed anyone, that the house belonged to the city anyway. Every fraudster is like that: he always has some explanation for himself.

— Did you know that Zosia was still alive? — I asked. — And Auntie Halina?

— I knew. — Edward Lis lowered his eyes. — I also knew they didn't talk to each other. That neither of them would fight. Zieliński knew that too. From me.

Then he said something I didn't expect.

— And Patryk… he's my nephew. My brother's son. He was a good boy when he was little. Then drugs, bad company, prison. Zieliński took him on for “security”. I gave him Patryk's number. Me. Do you understand? I dragged him into it. When I heard on television about the fire on Lipowa Street, that a man had been sleeping in the cellar, that he almost died… — Edward Lis started coughing, for a long time, terribly. Kasia gave him some water. — I'm not a good man. But I'm not a murderer. And Patryk wasn't meant to be a murderer either.

— Mr Edward — said Kasia very quietly. — Will you say all this in court?

He was silent for a long time. He was looking at the calendar from two thousand and twenty-three.

— I'm going to die anyway — he said at last. — Better to die as someone who told the truth than as someone who stole a house from two old women. Yes. I'll say it.

---

On Wednesday evening Tomek called from the police station in Wola.

— We've got him. Patryk. He was sitting in a mate's garage, with a rucksack and a fake passport. He wanted to go to Germany tomorrow. In your honour, Jonas.

— And what does he say?

— Nothing so far. But when I told him his uncle would testify, he went very pale. I think he'll talk tomorrow. People like him always talk when they realise nobody will protect them any more.

On Thursday Patryk Lis talked. Everything. That the break-in in November was ordered by one of Zieliński's men. That they were looking for the key and documents. That in the cellar he tried to break down the wall because Zieliński suspected there was something there: before he died, old Stanisław Nowak had supposedly told the neighbours that “the truth lies in the walls of this house”. That he got the petrol from the same man. And that this man told him: “The boss says: let it burn. If it burns down, nobody will prove there was anything there.”

The prosecutor said Patryk could count on a lighter sentence if he cooperated. Patryk denied only one thing: that he had wanted to kill anyone. Nobody believed him.

— Did Zieliński know that Mr Zbyszek was sleeping in the cellar? — Tomek asked at the interrogation.

Patryk was silent for a long time. Then he said:

— Everyone knew. The whole street knew.

---

The train is slowing down. The first blocks of Kraków appear outside the window, then the chimneys of Nowa Huta in the distance, then church towers. Kasia wakes up. Tomek closes his laptop and says he'll finish the report tomorrow, or next year.

An application for the arrest of Ryszard Zieliński is already lying at the prosecutor's office in Kraków. Tomek says the prosecutor will sign it on Monday at the latest.

— Why not today? — I asked.

— Because prosecutors have weekends too — said Tomek. — And Zieliński has very good lawyers. Everything has to be perfect.

I hope Zieliński doesn't know what we know. But Auntie Halina would say that people like that always know.
