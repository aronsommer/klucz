# Chapter 17. New Year's Eve

_Friday, 1 January 2027, four in the morning_

A new year. I'm writing in the kitchen, in my coat, because I haven't had time to take it off yet. Cold hands and a warm heart. I think that's what you say. If not, from today that's what you say.

One thing at a time.

---

For four days after the talk with Auntie Halina (I still can't get used to that word) I hardly slept. I thought about Grandma. About the girl who wrote “LIARS” on the university wall, and about the other girl who sat for nine hours in a room without a window. I tried to understand what I would have done in her place. I don't know. I'd like to say I would have been brave. But it's easy to be brave in two thousand and twenty-six, in a warm kitchen, with a laptop and a cat.

I talked about it with Kasia. She said something I'll remember for ever:

— You don't have to judge her. She has been judging herself for fifty years. The sentence has already been passed. Now she needs help getting out of prison.

---

On Thursday, the thirty-first of December, Michael called from Hamburg in the morning.

— Jonas, I have news for you. Good news. Very good. From February we need a new head of department. The board wants you. A bigger salary, your own team, a company car. There's just one condition: you have to be on site. In Hamburg. Five days a week.

I didn't know what to say. Half a year ago that would have been my biggest dream.

— When do I have to give an answer?

— By the end of January. Think it over. — Michael laughed. — And come back from that Poland of yours at last.

I put the phone down and looked out of the window for a long time. In the courtyard Mr Zbyszek was hanging lights on the tree. Bonifacy was sitting on the windowsill keeping an eye on him.

---

In the evening, before going out, I knocked on Auntie's door. She was sitting in front of the television in her new slippers, with Bonifacy on her lap and a plate of vegetable salad which, according to the doctor, she shouldn't eat.

— Mayonnaise is a medicine too — she said before I could say anything. — For the soul.

— Am I not right? — she added when she saw my face.

I brought her a small bottle of champagne. She said she would drink one glass at midnight, “for Zosia”, and the rest tomorrow, “for myself”.

— Go on, go — she waved her hand. — Young people shouldn't sit with old women on New Year's Eve. Just be careful of the firecrackers. Last year a boy in Kazimierz lost two fingers.

— Auntie, and the letter…

— Not yet. — She didn't look at me. — But it's lying on the table now, not in the drawer. That's some progress, isn't it?

---

There was a big concert on the Main Square. Kasia says tens of thousands of people come here every year. I believe her. I had never in my life seen so many people in one place. A stage in front of the Cloth Hall, huge screens, lights in every colour. The bands played one after another. I didn't know any of them, but everyone around knew every word and sang along. Fans were jumping and shouting. The most famous band was to play at midnight, and its performance was being shown on television across the whole country. It was minus seven. Nobody minded.

We were a big group: Kasia, Tomek, Magda, the guy from Portugal (his name is Rui and since St Andrew's Eve we've been mates), two of Magda's friends and Tomek's new girlfriend, Ewelina, who works in the fire brigade and has the strongest handshake I know. Even stronger than Tomek's.

Magda organised everything: a spot near the stage, champagne, plastic glasses and hats with “2027” on them.

— Today we have to have fun — she said. — Tomorrow we'll worry. Don't go anywhere without me, because among so many people we'll never find each other.

Kasia's friends are very different. Magda is intelligent and sharp, but nervous. Rui is helpful and as loyal as a dog. Paweł is mysterious, and his partner is sensible for both of them. Ewelina is brave, does three sports and has one basic rule: no stupid questions before midnight. And me? Magda says I'm a rare species: a German who's late.

Kasia also told me that on New Year's Eve you have to wear something red, preferably underwear, because it brings luck. I won't say whether I listened.

Almost fifty thousand people in one square. Kasia says last year there were just as many. Then the light shows began on the walls of the Cloth Hall.

— Whatever happens, it's the best party of the year — said Magda. — You genius, don't just stand there, dance!

— Roll on spring! — shouted Rui, who can't stand the Polish winter.

I had wanted to see something like this at least once in my life. I could hardly believe I was here.

Then we danced on the cold cobblestones. I don't dance very well, but nobody was looking.

At about eleven Magda suggested that everyone should say their New Year's resolutions. In Poland, just like in Germany, people make a list for the New Year of things they'll change in their lives. And just like in Germany, by February nobody remembers it.

Tomek started first.

— I'll lose five kilos. I'll go running three times a week. I won't eat after eight.

— After eight what? — asked Ewelina. — After eight sandwiches?

Everyone laughed, Tomek the loudest.

— Do you train at all? — asked Magda.

— From tomorrow. I'm joining a gym. I've been meaning to for three years.

Tomek weighs, as he says himself, “a few kilos too much for a policeman”. Ewelina said she had decided to help him: she'll go running with him at six in the morning. Tomek stopped smiling.

Then he talked about his football team. This season they lost every match except one, and that was only because the other team didn't turn up.

— On the pitch what counts is character, not the result — he said. — The winner is the one who comes back to training after every defeat.

— So who wins your matches? — asked Ewelina.

— The other lot. But we have more character.

Magda said that this year she would learn to play the guitar and wouldn't reply to her ex-boyfriend. Rui said he would pass the B2 Polish exam. Ewelina, that she would do a parachute jump. Nobody doubted she would do it.

Magda also said that her biggest dream was a year in South America, and that it was a challenge she finally had to take on. I thought that each of us has a future we plan and one that comes by itself.

Then it was my turn.

— I'll write my diary every day — I said. — Really every day. I'll learn all seven cases. I'll learn to dance. I'll read one Polish book without a dictionary. And…

I stopped.

— And? — asked Kasia.

— And I'll make one important decision. A good one.

Kasia looked at me, but didn't say anything. She had heard Michael call too. We were together when he called a second time in the afternoon.

— And you? — Tomek asked her. — What are your resolutions, sis?

Kasia was silent for a long time.

— I won't pretend — she said at last. — That's all. This year I won't pretend I don't feel something when I do.

Tomek whistled. Magda started clapping. I looked at Kasia and couldn't hear the music any more.

The new year was coming closer with every minute. People were pushing nearer the stage so as not to miss midnight.

---

At five to twelve everyone took out their phones and bottles of champagne. A huge clock appeared on the screens. People started counting together, loudly, tens of thousands of voices at once:

— Ten! Nine! Eight! Seven! Six! Five! Four! Three! Two! One!

— Happy New Year!

The sky over Kraków exploded. Fireworks, red, gold, green, silver, over the Main Square, over Wawel, over the whole city. People were kissing, hugging, opening champagne. Someone poured champagne over Tomek and Tomek was very happy.

Two thousand and twenty-six was over. Two thousand and twenty-seven had begun.

And Kasia turned to me and said:

— I'm not pretending.

And she kissed me.

Not on the cheek. Not three times. Once, for a very long time. Fireworks were exploding above us, people were shouting, someone was pushing me, someone was singing “Sto lat”, and I didn't hear or see anything.

When we finally pulled apart, Tomek was standing next to us and looking at us with a glass in his hand.

— Well, at last — he said. — I've been waiting for this since October. I lost a bet with Mum. Mum said by Christmas.

---

We walked home, because the trams weren't running and there were no taxis. We walked through the Planty, then along Starowiślna Street, holding hands. Music could be heard from every window. Empty bottles and bits of firecrackers lay in the streets. It was so cold that our ears hurt.

In front of my gateway Kasia stopped.

— Jonas. That offer from Hamburg.

— I haven't decided yet.

— I know. — She looked at me seriously. — I don't want you to decide because of me. Not for me and not against me. It has to be your decision. Deal?

— Deal.

— Good. — She smiled. — But if you were asking my opinion…

— Then?

— Then don't ask. Because I'll tell you.

She kissed me once more and went home. I watched her until she disappeared round the corner.

Earlier, at the gateway, she had also said:

— Some time we have to talk calmly. About us. I want there to be trust between us. Both ways. No games.

— Deal — I said. — That's fair.

— “Fair” isn't a Polish word.

— I know. But it's “ekstra”.

There was a light on in Auntie Halina's window. On the windowsill stood an empty champagne glass. And next to it, leaning against the pane, an open envelope.
