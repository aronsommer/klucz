# Chapter 4. The Man in the Black Coat

_Friday, 16 October 2026_

First day in the past tense. Kasia, if you're reading this: I tried.

A lot has changed during this week. The biggest change: I started thinking in Polish, at least in the shop. Yesterday I spent a hundred złoty at the supermarket and didn't say “sorry, I don't understand” even once. Kasia says I must appreciate every small success and that I mustn't get upset, because it leads nowhere. I'll try. For now I'm pleased with myself.

On Wednesday morning Mr Zbyszek came with Waldek and the new fridge. Waldek was short, fat and smoked cigarettes the whole time. The fridge got to the second floor after a quarter of an hour. Waldek said it was his record. But it was too big. It wouldn't go into the kitchen. Mr Zbyszek and Waldek tried for an hour. They shouted, pushed, argued, and once Waldek said a word I didn't know. Mr Zbyszek told me not to write it down. I didn't.

Later Kasia explained to me that it was the most common Polish swear word and that it's better not to use it. The milder versions are “cholera”, “kurczę”, “kurde” and “do diabła”. Mr Zbyszek mostly says “cholera jasna” or “this damn fridge”. Waldek mostly says “screw it”.

In the end they took the door off the kitchen and the fridge went in. Now I have a new fridge and a kitchen without a door.

— I'll do the door on Monday — Mr Zbyszek promised.

I don't believe him, but the fridge works.

Today at ten I had a meeting with the company online. My boss, Michael, was sitting in the office in Hamburg drinking coffee.

— Jonas, how's Poland? When are you coming back?

— I don't know. Maybe I'm not coming back.

— You will — said Michael. — Everyone comes back. But fine, work wherever you want, just finish the project by the end of the month.

I worked all day. Bonifacy slept on my keyboard. I wrote a lot of code and even more bugs.

My team in Hamburg is making a new version of a system for a big client, a transport company. I'm responsible for the data analysis: thousands of files, hundreds of tables, tests, tests and more tests. When the program works, nobody notices. When it doesn't work, everyone calls. That's what my job looks like. Michael says that if the project is a success, it will be a good step in my career. I think that if the project is a success, I'll finally get some sleep. It's only a matter of a few weeks.

The client is a big company with warehouses all over Europe. Their director wants the new system to control every delivery: every order, every product, every vehicle. The machines in all the warehouses are connected in one network. I have to start the program, enter the data from two old systems and remove the errors before the team in Hamburg can continue. When something doesn't work, I have to contact their IT guy in Rotterdam, who never answers the phone. Yesterday I forgot the password to my profile and lost an hour. That's business, says Michael. In this sector time is money.

Now Michael wants our system to use artificial intelligence. The model is supposed to predict by itself where and when a lorry will be needed. It sounds amazing, but in practice it means I have to teach a computer to think like a driver drinking coffee at a petrol station. Michael thinks it's an exceptional project. I think it's an exceptionally long project.

During the day Michael called three times. The first time to ask if everything was working. The second time to remind me about the deadline. The third time to say that the client expects results by next Friday. When I finally switched off the computer, it was already dark. I spent the whole day at home and didn't take a single private call. Nobody called privately.

---

In the evening I went for a walk to look at the city. Kazimierz at night is amazing. Small streets, old houses, lights in the windows, music from the bars. There were lots of young people in the square. They were drinking beer, eating zapiekanki and talking in different languages. I bought a zapiekanka. It was huge and very greasy. I ate the whole thing.

On the corner a boy was playing the guitar, and next to him an old couple was dancing. People were clapping. In front of one club there was a long queue to get in. Two girls asked me if I fancied a drink with them. I said that unfortunately not, because I was tired. All the way home I wondered why I had said that. I wasn't tired. I was just shy. In Polish and in German.

Some guy in a cap was selling obwarzanki and shouting that they were the best in the world. I bought one. It was cold and hard, but the guy had so much enthusiasm that I couldn't tell him the truth.

When I came back, a big black car was standing in front of our house. The door to the tenement was open. In the courtyard I heard voices. Mrs Halina was standing by the tree, and next to her stood a tall man in a black coat. He had grey hair, an expensive watch and very white teeth.

His car was huge and as black as a coffin. The man had the look of someone who is used to everyone listening to him.

— Mrs Halina, I only want to help — he was saying calmly. — This house is falling apart. The roof needs replacing, the windows too, there's no lift. You're eighty years old. I'll give you good money. You'll buy a new flat, with a lift, with a balcony…

— Never — said Mrs Halina. — I was born here and I'll die here.

— Nobody has to die. — The man smiled. But his eyes didn't smile. — Please think about it. I have time. But not very much.

— I'm offering you more than this miserable flat is worth — he added.

Then he saw me.

— Ah! You must be Mrs Zofia's grandson. — He came up to me and shook my hand. — Ryszard Zieliński. I'm very sorry about your grandmother. A wonderful woman.

— Did you know her?

— Everyone in Kazimierz knew her. — He gave me his business card. — If you want to sell the flat, please call. Any day, at any hour. I'll pay more than anyone else.

— I don't want to sell.

— Not yet — said Zieliński. — Good night to you both.

He got into the car and drove off.

---

Mrs Halina was standing by the tree and shaking. I don't know if it was from the cold or from anger.

— Is everything all right? — I asked.

— Nothing is all right. — She looked at me. Then she sighed. — Come and have some tea. It's cold.

It was the first time I had been in her flat. It was very clean and full of things. There were pictures on every wall, photos on every table, a cushion on every chair. The kitchen smelled of cake. Bonifacy went straight to his bowl.

Mrs Halina made tea and gave me a piece of plum cake.

— He's been coming for a year — she said. — At first he was nice. He brought flowers, chocolates. Now he's not nice any more. Mrs Wanda from the first floor sold him her flat in July. Mr Kazimierz from the third in August. There aren't many of us left. Me, Mr Zbyszek, the Nowaks on the ground floor, Ola and Bartek from the fourth floor. And you.

— He wants to get rid of us — she added. — One after another. First he smiles, then he promises, then he threatens. I know the type. But nobody will force me to move out of my own home.

— He tries to convince us that it's for our own good. But nobody will stop him if we don't. He wants to get the whole house. And I won't leave this flat. Unless I'm dead.

— He has properties all over the city. They say his company is worth almost a billion. But a billion doesn't buy respect.

— And my grandma? Didn't she want to sell either?

— Zosia? — It was the first time Mrs Halina had said anything about Grandma. — Zosia never sold anything. She left everything behind. People too.

I wanted to ask what that meant. But Mrs Halina stood up and said it was late.

At the door she also said:

— Mr Jonas. Don't show that key to anyone.

— You said you didn't know anything about the key.

— Because I don't know anything — she said and closed the door.
