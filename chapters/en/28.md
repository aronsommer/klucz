# Chapter 28. Letters

_Monday, 17 May 2027_

Kraków in May is a different city. The chestnut trees are blooming in the Planty, the lilacs on Wawel, and in the courtyard our tree, which, as it turned out, is a lime tree. That's why the street is called Lipowa: Lime Tree Street. I've lived here for seven months and only now did I realise it. Kasia says that's typical of men.

On Saturday Kasia and I went to the cinema, to a film festival. They were showing a series of old Polish films, and after the screening an actress who had acted in them forty years ago talked about her work. In the evening we went to the theatre to see a play based on a text by a famous writer. Kasia says it's a work in which every motif is a symbol, and the artist's vision is brilliant. I understood half of it, but I liked the music.

At the beginning of the month there was the “majówka”, the May holiday. The first of May is Labour Day, the third of May is Constitution Day, and the second of May, as Mr Henryk said, is “the holiday of nobody wanting to work between two holidays”. Half of Kraków went off to the lakes or the mountains. The other half had barbecues on their allotments. We had a barbecue in the courtyard. Mr Zbyszek, now without bandages, prepared sausages, pork neck and kebabs. Ewelina brought a salad. Tomek brought his appetite.

The renovation of the tenement is under way. The building crew arrives at seven in the morning and works until four. They drill, chisel, cut, carry out rubble, carry in sacks of cement and shout at each other across the whole courtyard. The cellar already has new stairs, a new door and new wiring. We left the place where the cavity was. Auntie wants to hang a small plaque there. She doesn't know yet what to write on it.

She says it has to be something special. That she's aware the plaque will stay there for a hundred years. That you always come back home.

The crew is led by Mr Mietek, who says everything as if he were giving orders in a war: “We knock this wall down! We dig here! We build here! Dig it out, take it away, mix the concrete!” Every morning his old car stops with a bang in front of the gateway, because its engine has probably done three hundred thousand kilometres, and one tyre is always almost flat.

The tenement is a listed building, so every change needs the conservation officer's permission. The aim of the renovation is to give it back its old appearance. The workers put up scaffolding, ran new cables and started replacing the windows. Every new window has to match the size of the old one. On the roof they made a protection against the rain. Mr Mietek says that on a building site what matters most is weight: whoever has the heavier hammer is right.

Mr Mietek chews gum the whole time and only speaks to the workers by shouting. The money for the renovation comes from the city, so every day Mr Henryk looks out of the window to see if the workers are really working. Yesterday I caught him counting them. He spotted me and pretended to be watering his flowers.

Mr Mietek has his technique: he jumps up the scaffolding like a monkey, takes off his helmet only for lunch and is never late. A courier delivers his materials every morning at seven. Over a hole in the courtyard the workers made a little bridge out of planks. Mr Mietek's approach to work is simple: first shouting, then coffee, then work.

---

On the tenth of May Edward Lis died. In hospital, in Warsaw, at night, in his sleep.

Auntie Halina went to the funeral. Alone. She said it was her business and her family, even if it's a family you don't choose. She came back in the evening very tired.

— There were five of us — she said. — The priest, two neighbours, some old army mate and me. They didn't let Patryk out of custody. — She sat down heavily on a chair. — I put one candle on the grave. A white one. He told the truth at the end. That's more than many people do in their whole lives.

Doctor Nowicka told Auntie that after a heart attack and emotions like these it would be worth thinking about therapy. Auntie said her therapy was cheesecake and talking to the cat. But she went to the first session. She says the psychologist is very nice and very young and that she has enough material for him for ten years.

---

And on Friday Mr Kaczmarek, the notary, called.

— Mr Weber, I have something for you that has been waiting for two and a half years. Mrs Zofia Weber left a letter with me in deposit. In November two thousand and twenty-four, when she was in Kraków for the last time. With the instruction: “To be handed to my grandson, Jonas Weber, when the house has been saved.” — The notary cleared his throat. — I wondered for a long time what “saved” meant. I think the court's decision is enough.

I went to his office on Saturday morning. The notary gave me an envelope and left me alone in his study. He closed the door. An old clock was ticking on the wall.

I'm copying this letter out here in full. To have it. And so that in twenty years, when I read this diary, I'll know where it all began.

---

> _Kraków, 14 November 2024_
>
> _My dear Jonas,_
>
> _if you are reading this letter, it means I am gone, that you have come to Kraków, that you have found Hala and that together you have opened what I locked with my dad in October nineteen seventy-one. It also means you are reading Polish. I knew you would learn. You have that stubbornness which in our family passes from generation to generation like an old wardrobe._
>
> _What happened to our family was not only a tragedy. First shock, then sadness, then a gulf between us that we couldn't cross. I called it bad luck. But it wasn't bad luck. All we lacked was understanding._
>
> _I have to apologise to you. All your life I kept silent. I spoke Polish to you when you were small, but I never told you about Poland. I didn't tell you about Kraków, about this house, about my parents, about my sister. I thought I was protecting you. Now I know I was protecting myself. Silence is a prison too. Sometimes worse than the one with bars._
>
> _Hala must have told you what happened in seventy-one. If she has, it means she is braver than she thinks. She was always braver than she thought. I just shouted louder._
>
> _I want you to know one thing: I forgave her very quickly. Already in Hamburg, in the first winter, when I was sitting in a strange flat, in a strange city, with a husband I was only just learning to love, I understood that if I had been sitting in that room without a window instead of her, I might have done the same. I wrote her a letter. She sent it back. Then twenty years went by, then thirty, and every year made it harder to take the first step. It seemed to me that if I had said nothing for so many years, I couldn't any more. That it was too late._
>
> _But it is never too late. It is only too late when a person dies. I understood that this year, when the doctor showed me my test results. I no longer have the strength to take that step myself. That's why I am sending you._
>
> _I achieved a lot in my life, but I wasted fifty years on silence. I dreamed of sitting at one table with Hala once more. I don't want to burden you, but I have to ask you: do me this last favour. I know you won't let me down._
>
> _I know this isn't fair. You are young, you have your own life, your work, your Hamburg. But I know you. When you were small and someone at nursery school was crying, you always sat down next to them and said nothing, you just sat there. Until they stopped crying. Hala needs someone who will sit down next to her and stay. I couldn't. You can._
>
> _In the dictionary I left you a note with four words. I wanted to teach you them, but I didn't manage to. So now I will explain them._
>
> _“Kocham”, I love — because it's the first word you need to know in any language._
>
> _“Tęsknię”, I miss — because there's no one good word for it in German, and for fifty years I did nothing else._
>
> _“Przepraszam”, I'm sorry — because this word is harder than it looks, and you have to say it before it's too late._
>
> _“Wybaczam”, I forgive — because this is the key. Not the iron one with the red ribbon. The real one. Only it opens what people lock up inside themselves._
>
> _I left the house to you, but it belongs to Hala just as much as to you. Look after it together. And look after each other. Hala will pretend she doesn't need anyone. Don't believe her. And don't let her eat too much vegetable salad, because she has a weak heart, just like our mother._
>
> _If you fall in love with someone in Kraków, and I have a feeling you will, because everyone falls in love in this city, don't wait. Don't lock anything up in a wall. Say it straight away._
>
> _I love you, my little one. I always did. Even when I couldn't say it in any language._
>
> _Your grandma Zosia_
>
> _P.S. Mrs Halina's cat is called Bonifacy. He is fat and conceited. But if he likes you, it means you're a good person. Cats know more than people._

---

I sat in the notary's study for half an hour. Maybe an hour. I don't know. The clock was ticking. Outside, on the Main Square, some band was playing, someone was laughing, pigeons were flying over the Cloth Hall. And I sat with the letter in my hands and cried like a little boy. For the first time since Grandma's funeral.

Then I went out onto the Main Square. The sun was shining so brightly that I had to close my eyes. The hejnał, the bugle call, was playing from the tower of St Mary's. The trumpeter broke it off halfway, as always, at the same place where almost eight hundred years ago a Tatar arrow hit the trumpeter in the throat. So the legend says. Kasia says it isn't true. But Kraków likes its legends. And it's right to.

I thought that in this city everything important gets broken off halfway. The hejnał. Grandma's studies. The conversation between two sisters. And then someone comes and finishes it.

---

When I came back from the theatre, I showed the letter to Auntie. She read it twice. Then she put it down on the table and looked out of the window at the lime tree for a long time.

— “Hala will pretend she doesn't need anyone” — she repeated. — She knew me. After fifty years she still knew me.

— She did.

Auntie smiled. Then she took from the plate the last piece of cheesecake, which she had baked herself and which she shouldn't eat.

— And I don't eat vegetable salad any more — she said. — Since yesterday.

Then I went to Kasia's. Without a text, without calling. Grandma wrote not to wait.
