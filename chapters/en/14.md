# Chapter 14. Snow

_Wednesday, 9 December 2026_

In the night the first snow fell. In the morning the silence woke me up. Kraków under snow is different: slower, quieter, as if someone had covered the city with a white tablecloth. Children were throwing snowballs at each other in the courtyard, and Mr Zbyszek was clearing the pavement and singing something very rude.

Bonifacy went out onto the balcony, touched the snow with his paw, looked at me reproachfully and went back to bed.

There was ice on the pavements. In the morning, on the way to the bakery, I slipped and fell right on my bum. Mrs Basia saw it through the window and came out with a sack of salt. She said the weather this year is crazy: twenty degrees in November, minus ten and a snowstorm in December.

Nine days have passed since the break-in. The police are looking for the man with the snake on his neck. So far without success. There is now a camera in the tenement, which Tomek installed. Mr Henryk says it's surveillance, but every day he asks if it has recorded anything.

---

In the afternoon Kasia and I went to the Christmas market on the Main Square. Wooden stalls, lights, the smell of mulled wine, gingerbread and grilled sausages. A huge Christmas tree in front of the Cloth Hall. Someone was playing carols on an accordion. It was cold, ten degrees below zero, but nobody was in a hurry.

I had to buy presents. Kasia said that in Poland at the Wigilia, the Christmas Eve supper, everyone gets something under the tree. Even people who come for the first time. Especially people who come for the first time.

— What should I buy your mum?

— Something for the kitchen. She loves anything for the kitchen.

— And Tomek?

— Something to eat. He loves anything to eat.

— And you?

Kasia stopped in front of a stall with gloves.

— Nothing for me. You mustn't ask what to buy. It spoils the surprise.

— That's not fair. You asked me about everything, and I can't ask about one thing?

— Life isn't fair — she said and bought me a bobble hat. An ugly one, striped, in the colours of the Polish flag. Now I have to wear it. That's the deal.

The market had everything: wooden toys, candles, wool scarves, Christmas tree decorations, honey, oscypek cheese from the mountains, and even Chinese goods pretending to be Polish handicraft. The sellers called out to tourists in English, to Poles in Polish, and to me, I don't know why, in Russian.

I bought Mrs Danuta a wooden chopping board with “Smacznego”, enjoy your meal, carved on it. For Tomek a huge gingerbread in the shape of a police car. For Mrs Halina warm woollen slippers and a new bowl for Bonifacy. For Mr Zbyszek a bottle of good cherry liqueur. I didn't buy Kasia anything while she was with me. I went back there later, alone, and bought a silver pendant in the shape of a small key. Maybe it's stupid. Maybe it's too early. But I've already bought it.

In the shop next door I bought myself some warm clothes for the winter. In the menswear department the saleswoman said that in this jacket I looked like a real Cracovian. Kasia said like a real tourist.

---

I was coming home with bags full of presents. It was dark on the staircase, because the light bulb had burned out again. On the first floor I tripped over something soft.

In my head I cursed the light bulb.

It was Mrs Halina. She was lying on the stairs, with a bag of shopping. Oranges had rolled all over the steps.

— Mrs Halina! Mrs Halina, can you hear me?

She opened her eyes.

— I'm all right — she said very weakly. — I felt dizzy. Only… it hurts here. — She put her hand on her chest.

I called the emergency number. One one two. My hands were shaking, but I spoke Polish clearly. The address, the floor, her age, what had happened. The dispatcher asked short questions: Is she breathing? Is she conscious? Does she have chest pains? Does she take heart medicine? She told me not to move Mrs Halina and to wait.

The ambulance came after eleven minutes. The longest eleven minutes of my life. The paramedics were calm and fast. They connected Mrs Halina to a machine, took her blood pressure, gave her a tablet under the tongue. They took her to hospital, to cardiology. I went with them.

In the ambulance the paramedic told me I had done the right thing not moving her. With a heart attack a quick reaction is what counts, and everyone should know how to give first aid. Hardly anyone does, he added. I learnt the basics on a course in Hamburg. I never thought they'd come in useful.

---

At the hospital I waited four hours in the corridor, on a plastic chair. Next to me sat a woman with her sick husband, a young guy with a broken arm and an older man who talked on the phone the whole time about the football results.

Opposite me sat a young pregnant woman. She was holding her belly and breathing deeply. Her husband was more frightened than she was. A nurse took them to the delivery ward. After an hour the husband came back alone, pale and happy, and said to everyone in the corridor: “I've got a son!” The older man stopped talking about the match for a moment and said: “Congratulations, sir. Sto lat!”

At last a doctor came out to me.

— Are you Mrs Kowalska's family?

I hesitated.

I didn't want to lie. But I didn't want to be left alone in the corridor either.

— Yes — I said. — I'm… her sister's grandson.

It was the first time I had said it out loud. The doctor didn't notice how strange it sounded.

— It was a heart attack. A small one, but serious. We did an angiography and put in a stent. Mrs Kowalska is now in intensive care, but her condition is stable. In a few days we'll move her to an ordinary ward. She was lucky that someone found her quickly.

— Was it very dangerous?

— Every heart attack is dangerous. With a sudden heart attack every minute counts. Actually, you saved her life. Now the most important things are treatment and rest. No stress.

— I'm afraid Mrs Kowalska won't want to rest — I said.

— I know — said the doctor. — Those are the best patients. And the worst.

— Is she conscious?

— She's sleeping now. It isn't a coma, it's the effect of the medicines. Under their influence a patient can sleep for a whole day. She didn't need an operating theatre or any cutting. We put the stent in through an artery in the arm. Medicine has changed a lot.

— And these medicines?

— They're strong. Side effects are rare.

— Can I see her?

— Tomorrow. Today she has to rest.

The news of Mrs Halina's heart attack spread through the tenement within an hour. When I came back at night, there were flowers from Mrs Grażyna outside her door, a cake from Ola and a note from Mr Zbyszek: “Fingers crossed. Good luck!”

---

_Thursday, 10 December 2026_

Today I was allowed to visit Mrs Halina. She looked very small in the hospital bed, with tubes and cables. But when she saw me, she immediately asked:

— Who's feeding Bonifacy?

— I am. Twice a day.

— You give him too much. I can see it in your face.

Then she asked me to bring her a few things from home. A nightdress, a dressing gown, her reading glasses, her rosary and the book from the bedside cabinet. She gave me her keys.

— The rosary is in the drawer, in the bedside cabinet. Just don't rummage through anything.

Her bedroom was quiet and smelled of lavender. I opened the drawer. The rosary was lying on top, wooden, old. Under it lay an envelope.

I didn't want to look. I really didn't. But I saw the handwriting on the envelope and I couldn't stop looking.

It was Grandma's handwriting. I know it from every birthday card, every shopping list, the note with the words in the dictionary.

_“Halina Nowak, ul. Lipowa 7, Kraków, Polen”._

The stamp was German. The postmark: Hamburg, 1972. And one more stamp, Polish, red: _“Addressee refused to accept. Return to sender”_.

Halina Nowak. Not Kowalska. Nowak.

The envelope was sealed. Nobody had ever opened it. But if it had been returned to Grandma, why is it in Mrs Halina's drawer?

I put the envelope back under the rosary. Exactly as it had been lying. I took the things to Mrs Halina at the hospital. I didn't say anything.

Kasia says I did the right thing. That it has to come from her. I don't know. Sometimes I think this whole family has been waiting for fifty years for someone to say something first.
