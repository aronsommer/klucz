# Chapter 6. Fever

_Tuesday, 20 October 2026_

Yesterday it rained all day, and of course I went for a long walk without an umbrella. I wanted to see Wawel. I saw Wawel, the dragon, the Vistula and a great deal of rain. I came home wet from head to toe.

In the night I woke up and I was terribly cold. Then terribly hot. Then cold again. My head hurt, my throat hurt, all my muscles hurt. I didn't have a thermometer, but I knew I had a fever.

There was fog in my head, my mind wasn't working. I felt as if my whole body was made of lead. I couldn't even reach for my phone.

In the morning I couldn't get out of bed. Bonifacy sat next to me and looked at me anxiously. Or he was hungry. With cats it's hard to tell the difference.

I wrote to Kasia that I wouldn't come to the lesson on Thursday because I was ill. After ten minutes she called.

— What's wrong with you?

— I don't know. Everything hurts. I have a fever. I'm coughing.

— Have you been to the doctor?

— No. I don't know where there's a doctor. I don't have a Polish… — I didn't know the word — that… document for the doctor.

— Insurance. — Kasia sighed. — OK. Lie down and don't do anything. I'll be there in an hour.

I lay there and thought it was funny: I came to Poland to find Grandma's past, and the first thing I found here was flu.

---

— Don't worry, you won't infect me — she said right at the door. — I have iron health.

Kasia came with a bag from the chemist's and a net of oranges.

She took my temperature. Thirty-nine point two.

— Well, great — she said. — I'll call my doctor. She has a private practice and sometimes makes home visits. You'll pay, but you won't have to stand in the queue at the clinic.

— If it gets worse, we'll call an ambulance — she added. — But first I want to make sure it's nothing serious. Have you got anything on your skin? A rash?

— No.

— Good.

Doctor Nowicka came in the afternoon. She was a short, energetic woman in glasses. She spoke fast and very clearly, as if she knew I was a foreigner.

— Please open your mouth. Say “aaah”. Good. Now please breathe deeply. Once more. Don't breathe. Good. Is the cough dry or wet?

— I don't know. Loud.

— Wet — she said to herself. — Since when have you been ill?

— Since last night.

— Are you allergic to anything? Are you taking any medicine?

— No and no.

— It's flu. Nothing dangerous, but you must stay in bed. No work and no walks for a week. Drink a lot: water, tea with lemon, with honey. I'll prescribe you something for the fever and a cough syrup. You don't need an antibiotic. If it isn't better in three days, please call me.

Doctor Nowicka also explained to me that flu is caused by a virus, so an antibiotic won't help. The body has to heal itself. The medicines are only for the symptoms: for pain, for fever, for the cough. The dose: one pill every six hours, no more. If the cough gets worse, it might be pneumonia, and then it will have to be treated differently.

Before that she took my blood pressure and looked in my ears.

— Your hearing is good — she said. — Your common sense is worse, since you walk around in the rain without an umbrella.

She wrote a prescription and left her card. Kasia went to the chemist's. I wanted to thank her, but I fell asleep before she came back.

---

When I woke up, someone was talking in the kitchen. Kasia and Mrs Halina. They were sitting at the table and drinking tea like old friends.

— He's awake! — said Mrs Halina. — I brought chicken soup. There's nothing better for flu. No pills, just chicken soup. My mother used to say so, and her mother too.

— Calm down and eat slowly. Hurrying does harm.

— I feel for you — she added. — Flu in a strange city is no fun.

The soup was golden and hot, with noodles, carrot and parsley. I ate slowly. Mrs Halina looked at me as if I were a small child.

— Under communism — she said — you had to queue for three hours for a chicken for soup. Meat was rationed. Mum cooked soup out of almost nothing: bones, a carrot, an onion, a bit of salt. She said it was soup made of patience.

— And during the war? — asked Kasia.

— I only know the war from stories. I was born just after it. But Mum told us about the hunger, the fear, the soldiers in the streets. In forty-four the Germans took Dad to a labour camp. He came back after a year, as thin as a stick. He never talked about it. — She looked at me. — It's not your fault. You weren't even born then.

— And later, in eighty-one?

— Martial law. The thirteenth of December. In the morning I turned on the television, and instead of a children's cartoon there was a general in dark glasses. Soldiers in the streets, cold, the phones didn't work. My husband said: “Hala, don't say anything, to anyone.” And I didn't say anything. I was always good at keeping quiet.

— Józek used to say that evil rules where people keep quiet. But back then silence was the only way to survive. Military patrols everywhere, guards at the factories, a curfew. A terrible time. And the reason was simple: people wanted to be free.

— Zosia always got ill in autumn too — she said suddenly. — Every year in October. And then in November she was as fit as a fiddle.

— Did you know my grandma well?

Mrs Halina looked out of the window. Outside it was already dark.

— I knew her. We lived in this house from childhood. She on the second floor, me… next door.

Kasia gave me a quick look. I didn't say anything.

— What was she like when she was young?

— Brave. — Mrs Halina smiled, for the first time really. — The bravest of us all. At school she always said what she thought. The teachers didn't like her. The boys loved her. In nineteen seventy, in December, when they were shooting at workers on the coast, she wrote “Liars” in big letters on the university wall. At night. Alone. The police searched for a month for whoever wrote it.

— And did they find her?

— No. Nobody said anything. — Mrs Halina stopped smiling. — Back then, nobody yet.

She stood up quickly, took the pot and said Bonifacy had to have his supper.

---

When she had left, Kasia didn't say anything for a while.

— Did you hear that? — she asked at last.

— “Back then, nobody yet”?

— Not only that. “She on the second floor, me… next door.” She wanted to say something else. Something like “me on the first” or “me with her”.

— Or “me at her place”.

— Or “me with her in one room” — said Kasia slowly.

We looked at each other.

— You've got a fever — said Kasia. — I think I'm starting to get one too. You need sleep, not riddles. Sleep. Tomorrow I'll come with more oranges.

Now it's night. I'm writing in bed, very crookedly. The thermometer shows thirty-eight. I'm thinking about the two girls in the photo in Grandma's room.
