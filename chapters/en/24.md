# Chapter 24. Easter

_Sunday, 28 March 2027, Easter_

“April weaves, mixing a bit of winter and a bit of summer,” says a Polish proverb. It's only the end of March, but the proverb is already working. On Monday it was twenty degrees and everyone was walking around in T-shirts. On Tuesday there was sleet. On Wednesday the wind was so strong that Mrs Grażyna's umbrella blew away. Today the sun has been shining since the morning, there isn't a single cloud in the sky, and the first green leaves have appeared on the tree in the courtyard.

Spring. The first spring of my new life.

---

A lot has happened over the last four weeks. I'll describe it briefly, because today is a holiday and I want to write about good things.

Mr Zbyszek came out of hospital after ten days. He has bandages on both hands and can't do anything, which for him is worse than the fire. He sits on the bench in the courtyard and tells others what to do. Ewelina visits him every week. She says it's an official visit. Mr Zbyszek says Ewelina saved his life and that if Tomek doesn't marry her, he will, even though he's sixty-two.

The cellar is closed. The building inspectors checked the structure and said the tenement is safe, but the cellar has to be renovated. The city will pay, because it's the city's part. Mr Henryk said the city never pays for anything. Mrs Grażyna said that this time it will pay, because there was an article in the newspaper. This time I think Mrs Grażyna is right.

The lawyer submitted copies of the documents from the deposit to the court. The company's lawyers asked for time “to familiarise themselves with the new evidence”. The lawyer says that means they don't know what to say. The hearing will be on the twentieth of April.

They haven't caught Patryk Lis yet. Tomek says he disappeared from Praga a week after the fire. But his fingerprints match the ones from my flat. There's a wanted notice out for him.

Zieliński is silent.

---

On Holy Saturday Auntie Halina taught me to make pisanki, painted Easter eggs.

The whole week before Easter Auntie was cleaning: she washed the net curtains, polished the silver teaspoons, arranged the tablecloths in the cupboard. She says that at Easter the house must be as clean as your soul after confession.

First you hard-boil the eggs. Then you paint them. You can do it in different ways. The simplest: paints with water. The traditional way: boil the eggs in onion skins, then they come out reddish brown. The hardest: draw patterns on the egg with hot wax, then put it in the dye, and then take the wax off. Auntie can do all the ways. I can do the first.

My pisanki look as if a five-year-old boy painted them. Auntie says they're “very modern”. One is blue with yellow dots. One is green with a little red heart. On one I wrote “KLUCZ”, but the letters came out crooked and it looks more like “KLUSZ”.

Then we prepared the little basket for blessing. A small wicker basket, inside it a white napkin with lace, and on it everything that is to be on the table for the Easter breakfast: pisanki, a piece of bread, salt, pepper, sausage, a piece of ham, horseradish, cheese and a little sugar lamb with a red flag. On top, a sprig of box.

On Holy Saturday everyone in Poland goes to church with their baskets, and the priest blesses them, that is he sprinkles them with holy water. The church in Kazimierz was full of people with baskets: grannies, children, young couples, students, even a few tourists who didn't know what was going on but came too. The priest was young and very energetic. He sprinkled us so hard that the pisanki were wet and my glasses were wet.

— That's a good sign — said Auntie. — The more water, the more blessing.

The priest blessed the baskets and said that Easter is a feast of hope. On the way home Auntie told me that for the first time in a long while she was celebrating in a family circle. That the bond that joins people at one table is sometimes stronger than blood. When she said it, she was looking at Kasia.

---

This morning there was Easter breakfast at Auntie's. Everyone came: Mrs Danuta with Kasia, Tomek with Ewelina, Mr Zbyszek in a new shirt (Ewelina buttoned it for him, because he couldn't do it himself), Ola and Bartek with little Zosia, the Nowaks. Mr Lewandowski sent flowers and a card with the wishes: “Happy Easter and a peaceful April.”

Auntie welcomed every guest at the door, like the hostess at a wedding. I served the żurek.

On the table: żurek with egg and white sausage, eggs with mayonnaise and cress, ham, pâté, ćwikła, that is beetroot with horseradish, the vegetable salad Auntie shouldn't eat, Easter babka and mazurek, a flat cake with a shortcrust base, with chocolate, dried fruit and nuts and jam. Auntie baked the mazurek. Mrs Danuta baked the babka. At the table there was a ten-minute discussion about whose cake was better. Mr Zbyszek won, because he ate both.

Before eating we shared an egg from the blessed basket, just as we shared the wafer on Christmas Eve. Wishes again. This time I didn't have to prepare, because I already knew how.

Then Auntie stood up and tapped her glass with a teaspoon.

— I have a request for you — she said. — I have a letter from my dad. He wrote it fifty-five years ago and on the envelope he wrote: “To be opened together.” Zosia is gone. But her grandson is here. And you're here, my… — she hesitated — my new family. I'd like Jonas to read this letter out loud. Here. Today.

She gave me the envelope. My hands were shaking just like at the wall.

Before I started reading, Auntie drew the curtains, although it was daytime. She said Easter is about light, but this letter is about shadow.

I read it.

---

> _Kraków, 23 October 1971_
>
> _My dear daughters,_
>
> _I am writing to you both, although at the moment you are not speaking to each other and I don't know if you will ever start again. I hope so. If you are reading this letter together, it means my hope was right, and I am happy, wherever I am now._
>
> _I have hidden the documents of our house here. I am not doing it so that one day you will get the tenement back. Houses come and go. I am doing it because the truth must not be lost. They can take our house, our work, our passports and our good name. But they cannot take from us what is true. The truth can only be hidden. And wait._
>
> _I am leaving this letter with the documents because I want you to know that I did all this knowingly. Despite what happened, and whatever people say, I have not stopped believing in you. May this letter come into your hands when you are ready to turn to each other. I have not told anyone this until now._
>
> _Zosia. You are the bravest person I know. You have your courage from your grandfather, your stubbornness from me and your heart from your mother. But remember that courage without mercy is as hard as stone. Nothing grows on stone._
>
> _Hala. I know what you did in January. I also know why. You did it to save me, Mum and Józek. I am not angry with you. I never was. I am angry with them, with those people in grey coats who make children choose between a sister and a father. That is their sin, not yours. Stop punishing yourself. I forgive you, but that is not enough. You must forgive yourself._
>
> _I am giving the key to Zosia, because she is leaving and needs something that will pull her back home. I am leaving the second key in the box, for Hala, because she is staying and needs something to wait for._
>
> _I love you both the same. It has always been so, although each of you thought it was the other one I loved more._
>
> _Your dad_

---

When I had finished, it was completely quiet at the table. Even little Zosia wasn't crying. Mrs Danuta was wiping her eyes with a napkin. Mr Zbyszek was looking out of the window. Mr Henryk took out a handkerchief and blew his nose loudly, and Mrs Grażyna, for the first time since I've known them, said nothing to him.

Auntie Halina was sitting very straight, with her hands on the table. She was crying, but quietly, without moving, only tears running down her face.

— He knew — she said at last. — For seven years, until his death, he knew and said nothing. He looked at me every day at dinner. And I thought he hated me.

Kasia stood up, walked round the table and hugged Auntie from behind. Auntie grabbed her hand and held it tight.

— “Stop punishing yourself” — Auntie repeated. — Fifty-five years too late, Dad. But all right. I'll try.

---

In the afternoon it was Wet Monday, although it wasn't Monday. Śmigus-dyngus is tomorrow, but Ola and Bartek couldn't wait and started today. In Poland on Easter Monday people pour water over each other. Children shoot with water pistols, boys drench girls, girls drench boys, and everyone together drenches random passers-by. Kasia says that in the old days boys threw girls into the river. Now a bucket is enough.

In the courtyard Tomek and Ewelina staged a real battle. Ewelina brought a fire extinguisher from the station, but Mr Zbyszek forbade it. They poured several buckets of water over each other. I got hit in the back by Kasia's water pistol. Auntie Halina stood on the balcony and watered everyone with a watering can, laughing like a little girl.

In the evening Mum called. From America.

— Jonas, how's Easter? It's raining here. Jim made a turkey, although it's not Thanksgiving. He said Easter is an occasion too.

He bought the turkey at the neighbour's farm.

I told her everything. About the letter, the box, Auntie. Mum listened in silence for a long time.

— You know — she said at last — your dad always said that Grandma had a locked room in her heart. And that he didn't have the key to it. It hurt him very much. Even in hospital, before he died, he told me he would like to go to Kraków with her one day. He didn't get the chance.

— Mum, will you come one day?

— To Kraków? — Mum laughed, but quietly. — Maybe. In summer. Jim always wanted to see Europe. And I'd like to meet this auntie who has the key to Grandma's room.

— She has her own key — I said. — With a green ribbon.

— Then give her my regards — said Mum. — And tell her she's always welcome here. Jim says there's only one ocean between Boston and Kraków, so it's close.
