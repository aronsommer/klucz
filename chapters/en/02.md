# Chapter 2. Bread, Milk and Mr Zbyszek

_Tuesday, 13 October 2026_

In the morning I wake up at six, because Bonifacy is sitting on my head. He's hungry. I'm hungry too, but there's nothing in the fridge. The fridge doesn't even work.

I get dressed and go out. On the stairs I meet a tall man in a blue jacket. He has big hands and a broken nose.

— Good morning! Are you new? — he asks.

— Good morning. Yes, I'm Jonas. I live on the second floor.

— In Mrs Zosia's flat! I know, I know. Zbigniew. But everyone says Mr Zbyszek. I've been the caretaker here for thirty years. If something doesn't work, come to me.

— The fridge doesn't work.

— There you go! We already have a problem. — Mr Zbyszek laughs. — I'll come in the afternoon. And where are you going now?

— To the shop. Where is the shop?

— On the corner there's a bakery, further on there's a greengrocer's, and in the square there's a supermarket. But bread only from Mrs Basia's bakery. In the supermarket the bread is like paper.

— Thank you!

— Don't mention it. And one more thing, Mr Jonas. — Mr Zbyszek makes a serious face. — Be careful with Mrs Halina.

— Why?

— Because she sees everything and knows everything. — He winks at me and goes downstairs.

Nice guy, I think. A bit strange, but nice.

— Mr Zbyszek! — I call after him. — Can I ask you something?

— Sure! But quickly, work is waiting.

— Where is there a cash machine?

— On the corner, next to the chemist's. But be careful, because sometimes it swallows your card. If there's any trouble, come to me. Anything. Always.

— Damn, this light bulb again! — he says to himself and looks at the lamp above the stairs. — People think a caretaker is an unimportant job. But without a caretaker the whole house would be pitch dark.

— And one more thing: we take the rubbish out to the container in the courtyard, and we fold up old boxes. Otherwise there's a mess and everyone shouts at the caretaker.

---

The bakery is warm and smells of bread. Behind the counter stands a short woman in a white apron. That must be Mrs Basia.

— Good morning. What can I get you?

— Good morning. One loaf of bread, please.

— Which one? Wheat, rye, wholemeal, with seeds?

I don't know these words. I point.

— That one.

— Rye. Here you are. Anything else?

— Yes. Those small… — I don't know what they're called.

— Rolls? How many?

— Four, please.

— A loaf and four rolls. Twelve złoty fifty groszy.

I pay by card. Mrs Basia looks at me with curiosity.

— You're not from here, are you?

— No, I'm from Hamburg. But my grandma was from Kraków.

— Oh, so you're Mrs Zosia's grandson! The whole street knows already. Mrs Halina told us.

Mr Zbyszek was right.

— Oh, you poor thing, all alone in a strange city! — says Mrs Basia. — Just a moment.

She disappears into the back room and comes back with two hard-boiled eggs.

— From a farmer near Kraków. A bit more expensive than in the supermarket, but wonderful. You'll like them.

— Oh, thank you. That's a great present.

— It's nice that you found us. Are you interested in bread? Because one day I can show you the oven. My grandfather built it, around nineteen sixty.

I also buy two cheese pastries. Mrs Basia gives me a third one for free, “as a welcome”. The pastries taste wonderful. I eat all three in the street, even though it's too cold to eat outside.

Then I go to a currency exchange, because I only have euros. On the board there are the rates: dollar, euro, pound, franc. The woman at the window counts the money very fast and says: “Please check it.” I check. Everything is correct.

---

At the greengrocer's I buy tomatoes, potatoes, onions and apples. At the supermarket I buy milk, butter, cheese, eggs, coffee and tea. And cat food. I don't want to have a cat, but it seems I already have one.

The supermarket has a huge choice. A hundred kinds of cheese, fifty kinds of sausage, chickens, fish, fruit from all over the world. It's similar in Germany, but here everything has a different name. I stand in front of the milk shelf for ten minutes, because I don't know what the difference is between one milk and another. Some kid looks at me and laughs.

There is a long queue at the till. The woman in front of me is talking on the phone very loudly. The man behind me says something to me. Again I don't understand.

— Sorry, I don't speak Polish well.

— I'm asking if you have a carrier bag — he says slowly. — A bag.

— I don't.

— Then you buy one. Over there. — He points.

People here are nice, but they speak very fast. I've had enough shopping for today.

My translation app says that “reklamówka” means an advert. The translation is wrong. I'll never trust it again.

---

In the café downstairs I drink a coffee and eat a roll with butter. The café is small, it has only five tables. There are pictures and notices on the wall. One notice is interesting:

> **Polish lessons for foreigners.**
> All levels. Fast, effective and with humour.
> Kasia, tel. 601 234 567

I write a text message.

> _Good morning, my name is Jonas. I'm from Germany. I want to learn Polish. Do you have time?_

The answer comes a minute later.

> _Good morning! I have time on Thursday at 5 pm. 12 Józefa Street, second floor. Your Polish is already quite good :) Kasia_

I smile. Thursday at five. Good.

I write back: “Ok, super! Thank you.” I send it before I have time to think. But I've already sent it. Too late. Then I wonder if “super” isn't too casual a word for a teacher. Kasia writes back with one word: “Great!” I guess not.

On my way out I ask for the bill. The waitress says that in this café you pay at the bar. One more rule I didn't know.

---

In the afternoon Mr Zbyszek comes. He looks at the fridge, then at me, then at the fridge again.

— This fridge is older than me — he says. — You need to buy a new one.

— How much does a new fridge cost?

— A lot. But I have a friend. My friend has a shop. My friend will give you a good price.

I don't know if it's a good idea, but I say: “All right.”

— Then wait until tomorrow — says Mr Zbyszek. — My friend will come in the morning. He's an old mate of mine, Waldek. He'll sort everything out.

— We always come to an agreement with Waldek — he adds. — I know nothing at all about fridges, but he does.

— And if the new fridge doesn't work either, I'll drop by with Waldek again. We'll talk, we'll figure something out.

Before Mr Zbyszek leaves, he sees the old key on the table. He picks it up and suddenly becomes very serious.

— Where did you get this?

— From my grandma. Do you know what it opens?

Mr Zbyszek is silent for a long time.

— I don't know — he says at last. — But I know this key. I once saw it at Mrs Halina's.

And he leaves.
