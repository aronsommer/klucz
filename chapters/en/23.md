# Chapter 23. The Box

_Monday, 1 March 2027, evening_

At twelve noon on the dot nine people were standing in the burnt cellar. Mr Kaczmarek, the notary, in an elegant coat and rubber boots he had borrowed from the firefighters. Mr Lewandowski, the lawyer, with a walking stick, like me. Tomek in uniform. Sergeant Majewska with a camera. The photographer from the “Gazeta Krakowska”. Mr Henryk as a witness, because, as he said, “someone independent has to make sure you don't cheat”. Kasia. Auntie Halina. And me.

The firefighters had set up lamps. In their white light the black cellar looked like a stage in a theatre.

The notary opened a folder and began to read. He spoke slowly, solemnly, in the language I already know a little from the offices:

— On the first of March, at twelve o'clock, in the cellar room of the building at seven Lipowa Street in Kraków, I, the notary Andrzej Kaczmarek, at the request of Mrs Halina Kowalska, née Nowak, and Mr Jonas Weber, in the presence of the witnesses named below, draw up a report on the opening of a wall cavity and the container found in it…

The photographer took photos. Sergeant Majewska took photos. Mr Henryk took photos on his phone. Everything was recorded, measured and described: the size of the cavity, the size of the box, the state of the wall, the time.

Then the notary looked at me.

— Please, Mr Weber.

---

I took the box out of the cavity. It was heavier than I had thought. Cold. I put it on the little table the firefighters had brought. My hands were trembling.

I was as excited as a child on Christmas Eve. My imagination showed me all kinds of pictures: gold, weapons, a treasure map.

Everyone gathered round the table. Mr Henryk stood on tiptoe to look over my shoulder.

I took the key out of my pocket. The red ribbon was a bit dirty from the smoke.

— Together — said Auntie Halina suddenly. She came over and put her hand on mine. — Dad said: for both of you.

We put the key in the lock. Together. The key went all the way in. I tried to turn it. It wouldn't. The lock was old, rusty, fifty-five years without moving. Mr Henryk said: “I told you so.” Mrs Grażyna, who was standing on the stairs although she shouldn't have been, said: “Henryk, quiet.”

I tried once more, with all my strength. Auntie pressed too.

Something inside grated. And the key turned.

Click.

The lid rose by itself, by a few millimetres. There was complete silence. For a moment nobody breathed.

---

Inside, everything was wrapped in oilcloth, old, yellow, checked. The kind people used to put on the kitchen table. Under the oilcloth, in grey paper, lay four things. The notary took them out one by one, in white gloves, and described each one for the report.

When Mr Henryk wanted to touch the papers, the notary gave him a sharp look. Auntie sent me a smile.

**The first:** a thick envelope marked “House documents”. Inside, a notarial deed from nineteen twenty-six, on thick paper, with seals, in which Jan Nowak, merchant, buys the tenement at seven Lipowa Street. And Jan Nowak's will from nineteen forty-nine, written in his own hand, signed in front of two witnesses.

The notary read one sentence out loud:

_“All my property, and in particular the house at no. 7 Lipowa Street in Kraków, I leave to my only son, Stanisław Nowak, and after him to his children.”_

— “Only son” — Mr Lewandowski repeated. Very quietly. Then once more, louder. — Only son. Mr Kaczmarek, please put that in the report word for word.

Mr Henryk stopped taking photos.

— The document is in perfect condition — said the notary. — Very valuable. After the report I'd like a word with Mrs Kowalska in private.

— Incredible — the notary said quietly. — Thirty years of practice and I've never seen anything like it.

**The second:** Stanisław Nowak's will from October nineteen seventy-one. Short, on one sheet. Everything he had and everything that was ever due to him he left “to my daughters, Zofia and Halina, in equal parts, on condition that they come for it together”.

Auntie Halina covered her mouth with her hand.

**The third:** a photograph in a dark wooden frame. The whole family, around nineteen fifty, in the courtyard under the tree. An older man with a moustache, in a hat: Jan Nowak. A tall, thin man in glasses: Stanisław. A pretty woman with dark hair: Maria. And two little girls in the same dresses. The older one is holding the younger one's hand.

**The fourth:** two letters, in envelopes. On one envelope was written in Grandma's hand: _“For Hala”_. On the other, in handwriting I didn't know, the plain, technical handwriting of an engineer: _“For my daughters. To be opened together.”_

And one more thing. At the bottom of the box, in a little velvet pouch, lay a second key. Just like mine. Only the ribbon was green.

Auntie Halina took it in her fingers. She looked at it for a long time.

— Green — she said. — Mum always said green was my colour. The colour of hope. — She looked at me. Tears were running down her cheeks. — The fourth candle, Jonas. Always green. I didn't know why. Now I know.

Next to the key there was also a gold ring with a small diamond. Auntie said it was her mother's engagement ring. She thought it had been lost in seventy-one. The most valuable object in the whole box. The notary wrote in the report: “a ring of yellow metal with a stone, probably a diamond”.

---

The notary wrote everything down in the report. At the end he read the whole report out loud, word by word, so that everyone could understand it before signing. Auntie, the witnesses and I signed. The house documents, that is the deed and both wills, the notary took into notarial deposit, so that nobody could destroy them or challenge them. The photograph, the letters and the second key he left with us.

Before he left, Mr Lewandowski shook my hand. Long and hard.

— Mr Weber, I've been practising law for over sixty years — he said. — I've rarely seen such beautiful evidence. One sentence, written in a dead man's hand, in a will almost eighty years old, walled up in a wall. No expert will challenge it. No lawyer of Mr Zieliński's will explain it away. “Only son.” Antoni Nowak has just died a second time. This time for good.

Tomek had something in his eyes that looked like tears. He said it was from the smoke.

---

In the evening we sat at Mrs Danuta's, because there's no electricity in the tenement and the firefighters haven't let us go back yet. Auntie Halina had the letter from her dad on her lap. She didn't open it.

— He wrote: “to be opened together” — she said. — Zosia is gone. But you're here. We'll do it together. Only not today. Today I'm tired. Fifty-five years is a long time.

The letter from Grandma, the one marked “For Hala”, Auntie put in her handbag. Next to that letter from seventy-two. Now she has two letters from her sister. One she has already read. The other, she says, she'll read in a place she'll choose herself.

Kasia sat next to me on the sofa, holding my hand in one hand and the photo of the Nowak family in the other.

— Do you know what's strangest? — she said. — That all this time, for fifty years, everything was lying there, a metre from the stairs. People walked past it to get potatoes. Mr Zbyszek swept that floor for thirty years. And the truth was behind a dozen bricks.

— That's how it always is with the truth — said Auntie Halina, without opening her eyes. — It lies close by. You just need the courage to break down the wall.
