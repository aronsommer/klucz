# Chapter 26. The Chase

_Sunday, 11 April 2027, at night_

The prosecutor didn't get round to signing the application. Zieliński was faster.

Yesterday evening, Saturday, at about nine, I was sitting at Auntie Halina's. We were playing cards. Auntie plays cards the way she lives: honestly, but without mercy. I was losing my third game in a row when someone rang the doorbell.

Auntie looked through the peephole and became very calm. Too calm.

— It's him — she said.

— Don't open the door.

— I will. Let him look me in the eye. — She tidied her hair. — And you sit in the kitchen and record. Have you got a voice recorder on that phone of yours?

I had. I switched it on. I sat in the kitchen with the door ajar.

---

Ryszard Zieliński came in with a bunch of white roses. Elegant as always: a dark coat, a grey scarf, expensive shoes. But something about him was different. He wasn't shaved. There were shadows under his eyes. His hands shook slightly as he held out the flowers to Auntie.

— Mrs Halina. I'm sorry about the late hour. We need to talk like grown-ups.

— Go ahead. — Auntie didn't take the flowers. Zieliński put them on the table.

— I know what happened in Warsaw. I know about Edward. — He sat down, although Auntie hadn't invited him to. — Edward is a sick man. He has cancer, he takes strong medicine, he doesn't know what he's saying. No court will believe him. But the trial will be long, expensive and exhausting. For you too. At your age, with your heart…

— Get to the point, Mr Zieliński.

— Fine. To the point. — He took an envelope out of his pocket and put it next to the roses. — Three million złoty. For your flat and for your share of Stanisław Nowak's estate. In cash or by transfer, as you prefer. You withdraw your statements, your sister's grandson does the same, and I forget the whole matter. You'll buy yourself a house by the sea. Or in the mountains. Or in Spain. There'll be something left for that boy.

— I sense you're a sensible woman. Don't be taken in by fairy tales about justice. I'll grab this house one way or another. Better that you get something out of it.

Auntie Halina didn't even look at the envelope.

— Mr Zieliński — she said. Very slowly. Very clearly. The way she spoke to me in the first week, when I didn't understand anything. — Fifty-six years ago two men in grey coats came to me. They were polite. They spoke calmly. They offered me something too. That time I agreed. Because of it I lost my sister. For my whole life.

Zieliński wanted to say something. Auntie raised her hand.

— I won't agree a second time. Not for three million. Not for thirty. Take your flowers and your envelope and get out of my home.

For a moment it was completely quiet. I could only hear something humming in the fridge.

Then Zieliński stood up. And he wasn't elegant any more.

— You stupid old… — he began. His voice was completely different now. Low, cold, ugly. — Do you think some piece of paper from the cellar will save you? I have people in the court. I have people in the city office. I have people everywhere. I'll destroy you. You, that German, his teacher, that policeman, all of you.

He banged his fist on the table so hard that the roses fell on the floor.

— You've just threatened me — said Auntie calmly. — In front of a witness.

I couldn't stand it any more. I came out of the kitchen with the phone in my hand.

— I recorded everything — I said. — From beginning to end.

Zieliński looked at me. Then at the phone. Then at me again. And suddenly he lunged. He wanted to snatch the phone from me. He hit me in the face with his elbow, I tasted blood in my mouth, but I didn't let go. Auntie was screaming. Bonifacy, who had been asleep on the wardrobe, jumped straight onto Zieliński's neck and dug his claws into his head.

Zieliński yelled, threw the cat off, shoved me against the wall and ran out of the flat.

It all lasted maybe five seconds. First the blow to the face, then the bang against the wall. Zieliński wanted to kick me as well, but he didn't have time.

He hit me so hard that he almost knocked out a tooth.

---

I ran after him. I know. My leg was weak, and my walking stick was still in Auntie's hall. But I wasn't thinking. I ran.

In the courtyard Mr Henryk was standing at his window shouting something about the police. Zieliński ran through the gateway. His car wasn't in the street. Later it turned out that the driver had driven off when he heard the shouting. Zieliński turned left, towards the Vistula.

Kazimierz on a Saturday evening is full of people. Tourists, students, couples, groups of lads on stag nights. Zieliński ran between them, pushing people, knocked over a table in front of some bar. I ran after him shouting in Polish: “Catch him! Thief!” People turned round, but nobody caught him. One girl was filming us on her phone and shouting: “Come on, come on!” I don't know at whom.

I followed him as fast as I could. Every so often he disappeared into the crowd. He ran across the tram tracks before the next tram came.

I chased him along Szeroka Street, across Plac Nowy, past the round hall where they sell zapiekanki. Zieliński jumped out into the street right in front of a tram. The driver braked and rang the bell, and I ran behind the tram before I had time to think. The whole escape lasted maybe five minutes. For me it was an eternity.

Don't give up, I kept telling myself. Don't give up.

While running I called Tomek. I think I wasn't making sense: “Zieliński… running… Vistula… Józefa…” But Tomek understood.

— Where exactly?

— Szeroka… now down… to the riverside.

— He's running to the footbridge — said Tomek. — To the Bernatka. I'm in a police car in Podgórze. We'll block him from the other side. Don't go near him, do you hear? Don't go near him!

I went near him.

---

The Bernatka footbridge is a bridge for pedestrians and cyclists between Kazimierz and Podgórze. Above its middle, metal figures of acrobats hang on cables. Thousands of padlocks hang on the railings. Lovers come here, hang a padlock with their names on it and throw the key into the Vistula. Together forever.

Zieliński ran onto the footbridge. Halfway across he stopped. On the other side, from Podgórze, a police car with blue lights pulled up. Two police officers and Tomek got out. They ran towards him.

— Stop! Police! — shouted Tomek.

People on the footbridge hardly reacted. Only some guy shouted to me: “Don't go after him, mate, he'll attack you!”

Zieliński turned round. Behind him was me. I was limping, bleeding from the mouth, barely breathing. But I was there.

He was trapped. Between me and the police, above the black water.

He took a phone out of his pocket. His own phone. He raised his hand over the railing. I understood what he wanted to do. That phone probably had conversations with his people, messages, numbers. Everything the prosecutor needs.

I threw myself at him. I grabbed his hand. The phone fell from his fingers, bounced off the railing and… stopped. It got stuck between two padlocks. One was red, in the shape of a heart. On the other was engraved: “Ania + Michał, 2019”.

Then Tomek got there. Zieliński still tried to break free, but Tomek has hands like stone. Ten seconds later Ryszard Zieliński was lying on his stomach on the footbridge, his hands in handcuffs, among thousands of padlocks.

— Ryszard Zieliński — said Tomek, a bit out of breath. — You are under arrest on suspicion of inciting arson, attempted murder, fraud and forgery of documents. You have the right to refuse to make a statement. You have the right to a lawyer. Please stay calm.

— I have the best lawyers in Poland — said Zieliński into the concrete.

— Good — said Tomek. — You'll need them.

— People like him attack when they're trapped — Tomek told me afterwards. — They turn violent.

— We've got him — Tomek said into his radio. — On the footbridge. Caught red-handed.

One of the police officers carefully took the phone out from between the padlocks, wearing gloves, and put it in an evidence bag.

As they were leading him to the police car, Zieliński turned to me.

— You set me up — he kept repeating. — You all set me up.

— Do you think you've won? — he hissed. — You're nobody. A German with a key.

I thought for a moment about what to answer. In Polish. Correctly.

— I'm Zofia Nowak's grandson — I said.

---

Now it's two in the morning. I'm sitting in the hospital's A&E, because Kasia said that if I didn't show the doctor my lip and my leg, she'd kill me. My lip is split, it needs two stitches. My leg hurts, but it's in one piece. Kasia is sitting next to me holding my hand. First she shouted at me. Then she cried. Then she shouted again. Now she's quiet and just looking at me.

I know I acted stupidly. It was complete madness. But I don't regret it.

Auntie has called four times already. Bonifacy is a hero. Auntie says that from today he gets nothing but beef. Mr Zbyszek sent a text: “Bravo Mr Jonas!!! Next time I'll do the running!!!” Mr Henryk sent a text: “I filmed everything from the window. Something is changing in this country after all.”

— You know — Kasia said a moment ago — that lovers' padlocks hang on the footbridge? And the keys are thrown into the Vistula.

— I know.

— I wouldn't throw mine in. — She touched the small silver key round her neck. — I prefer to keep the key with me. In case I want to open something. Or lock it.

— That's very un-Polish of you.

— No. — She smiled for the first time that night. — That's very Nowak.
