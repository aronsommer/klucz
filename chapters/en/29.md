# Chapter 29. The Sea

_Saturday, 17 July 2027, Sopot_

I'm writing on the beach. There's sand in my notebook, in my pen, in my shoes, in the sandwiches and probably in my thoughts too. The sun is shining, a light wind is blowing from the sea, seagulls are screaming overhead. Next to me, on the blanket, Kasia is asleep in a big straw hat. She has a ring on her finger.

But one thing at a time. That's what Kasia says: “Write things in order, otherwise nobody understands anything afterwards.”

---

Mum flew into Warsaw with Jim on Monday. They came to Kraków by train on Tuesday. At the station Mum hugged me for a long time and said I had lost weight. For the first time in my life someone in Poland told me I had lost weight, and not that I was thin. Mrs Danuta will be delighted.

Their plane landed an hour late, because the pilot had to wait a long time for permission. Jim's luggage got lost and only turned up again in Kraków. Jim says he has travelled all over the world and his luggage always travels separately, as if it had a life of its own.

Jim is tall, cheerful, bald and wears shirts with Hawaiian flowers. On the plane he learnt three Polish words: “dzień dobry”, “dziękuję” and “pierogi”. He uses all of them at every opportunity, sometimes in one sentence. When he doesn't understand something, he says “uh” or “oh” and smiles. Mrs Danuta adores him. Grandma Tereska said he was “a decent American, just loud”.

Mum and Auntie Halina met for the first time on Wednesday. In Grandma's flat, at her table. I was very afraid of that meeting. There was no need. Mum came in, looked at Auntie and said in English:

— You have her eyes.

Auntie doesn't know English. But she understood. They hugged and stood like that for a very long time. Bonifacy walked round them three times, as if checking that everything was all right.

At supper Jim talked about politics in the United States: about the governor of his state, about the senators and about how in his town every state official is a friend of his from high school. Auntie said it's the same in Poland, only nobody admits it.

---

On Thursday we went north. Auntie, Mum, Jim, Kasia and me. By train, across the whole of Poland, from south to north, six hours. Outside the window the country changed: first hills, then plains, fields of grain yellow from the sun, pine forests, lakes, small stations with flowers in pots, storks on poles. Jim took a photo of every stork. Every hour Auntie took food out of her bag: egg sandwiches, ham sandwiches, cheese sandwiches, tomatoes, cucumbers, apples, yeast cake. In Poland nobody gets on a train without food for three days.

On the first day by the sea we spent a long time looking for a parking space, because Jim had hired a car that was too big for Polish streets. Then we packed the beach basket: a blanket, towels, sandwiches, juice. Auntie also took an umbrella and a thermos of tea, because “by the sea you never know”.

We walked to the beach by a shortcut through the forest. We came back by taxi: the driver took us right to the guest house, and the next day drove us to Gdańsk. Jim paid by card everywhere, counted every cent and said that bucks, euros or złoty were all the same magic to him. In Boston he would have paid a hundred bucks for a dinner like that. A boat steered by an old fisherman was sailing on the sea, and on the pier some boy lost his balance and almost fell into the water. Auntie was standing next to him, leaning on the railing.

Gdańsk is beautiful in a different way from Kraków. More maritime, more open, more… German? I don't know if I'm allowed to write that. Colourful houses with narrow fronts, the Long Market, the Neptune fountain, the old wooden crane over the Motława river. Amber shops everywhere. Kasia says amber is the tears of the sea. Jim bought Mum a necklace. I bought Auntie a brooch in the shape of a lime leaf.

On the Long Market stands Artus Court, where merchants from all over Europe used to do business. Jim said it was a fantastic place. Boats on the Motława were tied to the bank, and tourists were chatting away in every language in the world.

On Friday morning Auntie asked us to go with her to one place. Only her and me.

---

The Gdańsk Shipyard. The gates in front of which in August eighty the workers began a strike and Solidarity was born. Next to them a big modern museum building, rust-coloured like an old ship. And in front of the gate a monument. Three tall steel crosses, each with an anchor at the top. The Monument to the Fallen Shipyard Workers. For those who were killed in December nineteen seventy.

For those whom television called hooligans and bandits.

Auntie stood in front of the monument for a long time, without a word. Then she sat down on a bench. She took an envelope out of her handbag. “For Hala”. Sealed for fifty-five years.

— Sit down next to me — she said. — And don't say anything. Just sit.

I sat down. Grandma was right.

Auntie opened the envelope. Inside there was only one sheet, written quickly, a bit crookedly, in pencil. Auntie read it for a long time. Then she handed it to me without a word. I'm copying it out here, because Auntie allowed me to:

> _Hala,_
>
> _in three weeks I'm leaving. Dad is walling up the box right now, and I'm sitting on the cellar stairs writing to you by candlelight. I'm furious with you. I'm furious with the whole world. But I have to tell you something before I leave, because afterwards I won't have the courage._
>
> _When I wrote “LIARS” on the wall in December, I wasn't only thinking of them. I was thinking of all of us. Of Dad, who knows everything and keeps quiet. Of Mum, who plays the piano so as not to hear the news. Of myself, because for four years at university I was afraid to say out loud what I think. Of you too. Of the whole country, where everyone knows and everyone pretends not to know._
>
> _You only did what everyone does. You said what they wanted from you. I'm not better than you. I just shout louder._
>
> _One day we'll go to Gdańsk together. To those workers. We'll light a candle for them. And then we'll be quits._
>
> _Your stupid sister_
>
> _Z._

When I gave back the sheet, Auntie took a candle out of her handbag. A red one, an ordinary one, the kind that stands in every cemetery in Poland. She lit it with a lighter, although her hands hardly obeyed her, and put it in front of the monument, among the wreaths and flowers.

— Quits, Zosia — she said quietly.

Then she stood up, straightened her dress and said she was hungry. And that she wanted fish and chips by the sea, right now.

---

This morning we came to Sopot by local train. Sopot is a small, elegant town right by the sea, between Gdańsk and Gdynia. It has the longest wooden pier in Europe: over half a kilometre out into the Baltic. The beach is wide, the sand white and fine, and the sea has a colour I can't name, either in Polish or in German. Something between grey, green and blue.

The water in the Baltic is eighteen degrees today. Jim said “Oh my God” and didn't go in further than his knees. Mum went in up to her waist and came out after a minute. Kasia swam far out, as if she were a fish. I went in slowly, shouting with every step, and the people on the beach laughed.

Jim said that in California the ocean is colder, but the waves are bigger. The Baltic has small waves and is shallow. Children were bathing by the shore, older people were lying on blankets, and further away, in the port of Gdynia, you could see ships, ferries to Sweden and white yachts. A small fishing boat was sailing on the sea. The whole coast smelled of salt, fish and sun cream.

He also said that from here to Sweden it's maybe a hundred nautical miles.

There are freshwater showers on the beach. Kasia said that after swimming in the sea you have to wash, because the salt dries out your skin. Jim said the Baltic isn't a sea at all, just a big cold swimming pool. It was a sunny day and the sunbeams were dancing on the water.

The Baltic isn't deep, but on the surface the waves can be treacherous. On the other side of the bay, to the north, you could see Hel, a long, narrow peninsula. Kasia says that on Poland's northern coast summer is short, but beautiful.

And then Auntie Halina got up from her deckchair.

She took off her dress. Underneath she had an old navy-blue swimsuit, which she had brought specially. She went into the water very slowly, carefully, step by step. When the water came up to her chest, she stopped. She looked at the horizon.

— The last time I swam in the sea was in July seventy — she said. — Here. In Sopot. With Zosia. We came by train for one day, without our parents' permission. Zosia swam to the buoy and back. I was afraid.

Then she ducked under and swam. Slowly, but straight. To the buoy. An eighty-year-old woman with a stent in her heart swam through the cold Baltic to a yellow buoy, and the whole beach watched. Kasia swam next to her, just in case. I stood in the water up to my waist and couldn't breathe.

Auntie touched the buoy with her hand. She shouted something I couldn't hear because of the wind. Then she turned back.

When she came out onto the shore, soaking wet, with her grey hair stuck to her head, she was shaking with cold and laughing like a girl. An older man on the blanket next to us started clapping. Then his wife. Then the whole beach.

— What did you shout at the buoy? — I asked when I had wrapped her in a towel.

— “Zosia, I can do it too!” — Auntie wiped her face. — Fifty-seven years too late. But I can.

---

In the evening Kasia and I went to the pier. Just the two of us. The sun was setting over the sea, red, huge. At the end of the pier there were lots of people with phones, all taking photos of the sunset. We found a spot by the railing, a bit to the side.

I'd had a small box in my pocket for three weeks. I carried it everywhere. On the train. In Gdańsk. At the monument. On the beach, in my shorts, which made me afraid to go into the water.

Grandma wrote: don't wait.

I didn't wait. I knelt down on the wooden boards of the pier. The people around immediately stopped photographing the sun and started photographing us.

— Kasia — I said. My voice was shaking. All the words I had learnt in nine months suddenly ran away. Only a few were left. — I… can't dance. I can't ski. I mix up the cases. I keep saying “ten kawa” instead of “ta kawa”. But I know one thing. I love you. Will you marry me?

I opened the box. Inside was a ring. Silver, simple, with a small green stone. Green. The colour of hope.

Kasia looked for a long time. At the ring, at me, at the setting sun. Then she touched the small key round her neck.

— Physics — she said.

— Kasia…

— Yes — she said. — Of course yes, you idiot. Yes, yes, yes.

The people on the pier clapped. Someone shouted “Gorzko! Gorzko!”, “Bitter! Bitter!”, like at a wedding, so that we would kiss. We kissed. The sun went down completely. The first star came out over the sea.

When we got back to the guest house, Auntie was sitting on the terrace with Mum and Jim, drinking tea. She looked at Kasia's hand. Then at me.

— Well, at last — she said. — Tomek has won the second bet with his mum.
